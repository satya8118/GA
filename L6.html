Seven Lectures on Field Extensions

Lecture 6
The Kronecker–Weber Theorem

Every abelian extension of ℚ is cyclotomic.

§1 The theorem

Theorem (Kronecker–Weber) Every finite abelian extension $K/\mathbb{Q}$ is contained in a cyclotomic field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$ for some $n\ge1$. Equivalently, the maximal abelian extension is $$\mathbb{Q}^{\mathrm{ab}}=\bigcup_{n\ge1}\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n).$$

Conjectured by Kronecker (1853), proved by Weber (1886). Modern proofs use class field theory; special cases are elementary.

Question 1 Why are the two formulations of the theorem equivalent?
Each $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$ is abelian over $\mathbb{Q}$ (§3), so $\bigcup\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{\mathrm{ab}}$. Conversely a finite abelian $K$ lies in some $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$, and every element of $\mathbb{Q}^{\mathrm{ab}}$ lies in a finite abelian subextension, so $\mathbb{Q}^{\mathrm{ab}}\subseteq\bigcup\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$.

§2 Cyclotomic polynomials

Definitions $\zeta_n=e^{2\pi i/n}$. The group $R_n=\{\zeta_n^k:0\le kprimitive ones has $|m_n|=\varphi(n)$, and $R_n=\bigcup_{d\mid n}m_d$. The $n$-th cyclotomic polynomial is $\Phi_n(x)=\prod_{\alpha\in m_n}(x-\alpha)$.
Basic identities $$x^n-1=\prod_{d\mid n}\Phi_d(x),\qquad n=\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d).$$ $\Phi_p(x)=x^{p-1}+\dots+x+1$; $\Phi_{2m}(x)=\Phi_m(-x)$ for odd $m$; $\Phi_{p^k}(x)=\Phi_p(x^{p^{k-1}})=\dfrac{x^{p^k}-1}{x^{p^{k-1}}-1}$.

Question 3 Deduce $\Phi_{p^k}(x)=\Phi_p(x^{p^{k-1}})$ and compute $\Phi_9$ and $\Phi_{10}$.
Roots of $x^{p^k}-1$ not roots of $x^{p^{k-1}}-1$ are exactly the primitive $p^k$-th roots, so $\Phi_{p^k}=\frac{x^{p^k}-1}{x^{p^{k-1}}-1}=1+y+\dots+y^{p-1}$ with $y=x^{p^{k-1}}$. So $\Phi_9=x^6+x^3+1$. And $\Phi_{10}(x)=\Phi_5(-x)=x^4-x^3+x^2-x+1$.

§3 Irreducibility and the Galois group

Theorem 1 $\Phi_n(x)$ is monic and irreducible in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$. Hence $[\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n):\mathbb{Q}]=\varphi(n)$.
Question 4 Prove Theorem 1.
Let $f$ be the minimal polynomial of $\zeta_n$. Show $f(\alpha^p)=0$ for primes $p\nmid n$ by reducing mod $p$ and using $x^n-1$ separable over $\mathbb{F}_p$.
Write $\Phi_n=fg$ with $f$ monic irreducible in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ (Gauss lemma), $f(\alpha)=0$ for a primitive $n$-th root $\alpha$. Let $p\nmid n$ be prime; $\alpha^p$ is again primitive. Claim: $f(\alpha^p)=0$. If not, $g(\alpha^p)=0$, so $f(x)\mid g(x^p)$, say $g(x^p)=f(x)h(x)$. Mod $p$: $\bar g(x)^p=\bar g(x^p)=\bar f\bar h$ (Frobenius), so $\bar f,\bar g$ share a factor, hence $\bar\Phi_n$ and therefore $x^n-1$ has a repeated root in $\overline{\mathbb{F}_p}$. But $(x^n-1)'=nx^{n-1}$ with $p\nmid n$ is coprime to $x^n-1$: contradiction.
Now any $a$ with $(a,n)=1$ is a product of primes $p_1\cdots p_k\nmid n$; applying the claim repeatedly, $\alpha^a$ is a root of $f$. So all primitive roots are roots of $f$, giving $f=\Phi_n$. ∎
Theorem 2 $\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)/\mathbb{Q})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^*=U_n$. In particular $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)/\mathbb{Q}$ is abelian.
Question 5 Prove Theorem 2.
Each $\sigma$ permutes roots of $\Phi_n$, so $\sigma(\zeta_n)=\zeta_n^{k}$ with $(k,n)=1$. The map $\sigma\mapsto k\bmod n$ is a homomorphism ($\sigma\tau(\zeta)=\zeta^{k_\sigma k_\tau}$), injective since $\sigma$ is determined by $\sigma(\zeta_n)$. Both groups have order $\varphi(n)$ (Theorem 1), so it is an isomorphism. ∎
Corollary $U_n$ is cyclic iff $n\in\{1,2,4,p^k,2p^k\}$ with $p$ an odd prime.

§4 Quadratic fields are cyclotomic

Lemma 1 (Gauss) For an odd prime $p$ put $p^*=(-1)^{(p-1)/2}p$ (so $p^*=p$ if $p\equiv1$, $-p$ if $p\equiv3\pmod 4$). Then $\sqrt{p^*}\in\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$.
Question 7 Prove Lemma 1 using the Gauss sum $\tau=\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}\chi(a)\zeta_p^a$, $\chi$ the Legendre symbol.
Compute $\tau^2$; substitute $b=ac$ and use $\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}\zeta^{a m}=p-1$ if $p\mid m$, else $-1$.
$\tau^2=\sum_{a,b}\chi(a)\chi(b)\zeta^{a+b}$. Putting $b=ac$: $\chi(a)\chi(ac)=\chi(a)^2\chi(c)=\chi(c)$, so $$\tau^2=\sum_{c=1}^{p-1}\chi(c)\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}\zeta^{a(1+c)}.$$ The inner sum is $p-1$ if $c\equiv-1$, else $-1$. Hence $\tau^2=\chi(-1)(p-1)-\big(\sum_{c}\chi(c)-\chi(-1)\big)=\chi(-1)p$, since $\sum_{c}\chi(c)=0$. And $\chi(-1)=(-1)^{(p-1)/2}$, so $\tau^2=p^*$ with $\tau\in\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$. ∎
Lemma 2 Every quadratic extension $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)$ ($d$ squarefree) lies in some $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_N)$.
Question 8 Prove Lemma 2 by cases.
$d=-1$: $\mathbb{Q}(i)=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_4)$. $d=2$: $\zeta_8+\zeta_8^{-1}=2\cos\frac\pi4=\sqrt2$. $d=-2$: $(\zeta_8-\zeta_8^{-1})^2=-2$, so $\sqrt{-2}\in\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_8)$.
$d>1$ odd, $d=p_1\cdots p_k$: by Lemma 1, $\sqrt{p_1^*\cdots p_k^*}\in\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_d)$ and $p_1^*\cdots p_k^*=(-1)^{\sum(p_i-1)/2}d$. If the exponent is even, $\sqrt d\in\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_d)$; if odd, $\sqrt{-d}\in\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_d)$ and $\sqrt d=\pm i\sqrt{-d}\in\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{4d})$.
$d<0$: $\sqrt d=i\sqrt{|d|}$ lies in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{4N})$ when $\sqrt{|d|}\in\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_N)$.
$d$ even, $|d|>2$: $d=\pm2d'$ with $d'$ odd; $\sqrt d=\sqrt{\pm2}\sqrt{d'}$ is a product of elements of two cyclotomic fields, lying in their compositum $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{8})\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{N'})\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{\mathrm{lcm}})$. ∎

§5 Reduction to cyclic $p$-power extensions

Proposition If every cyclic extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ of prime-power degree lies in a cyclotomic field, then every finite abelian extension does.
Question 10 Prove the proposition.
Let $G=\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})$. By the structure theorem, $G\cong C_1\times\dots\times C_m$ with $|C_j|=p_j^{a_j}$. Let $H_j=\prod_{i\ne j}C_i$ and $K_j=K^{H_j}$; then $\operatorname{Gal}(K_j/\mathbb{Q})\cong G/H_j\cong C_j$ is cyclic of prime-power order. Since $\bigcap_jH_j=1$, the Galois correspondence gives $K=K_1\cdots K_m$. By hypothesis $K_j\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n_j})$, hence $K\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n_1})\cdots\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n_m})=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_N)$ with $N=\operatorname{lcm}(n_j)$. ∎

§6 Cyclic extensions of prime-power degree

Structure of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{p^n})$ For odd $p$: $\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{p^n})/\mathbb{Q})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/p^n)^*\cong\mathbb{Z}/(p-1)\times\mathbb{Z}/p^{n-1}$. For $p=2$, $n\ge3$: $(\mathbb{Z}/2^n)^*\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\times\mathbb{Z}/2^{n-2}$. So for odd $p$ there is a unique subfield $K_n\subset\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{p^n})$ of degree $p^{n-1}$; it is cyclic and totally ramified at $p$.
Question 11 Identify the degree-3 subfield of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_9)$.
$\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_9)/\mathbb{Q})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/9)^*\cong\mathbb{Z}/6$, which has a unique subgroup of order 2, namely $\{\pm1\}$ (complex conjugation). Its fixed field has degree 3: it is $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_9)^+=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_9+\zeta_9^{-1})=\mathbb{Q}(2\cos\frac{2\pi}9)$, the cyclic cubic field of conductor 9.
Conductor theorem Let $K/\mathbb{Q}$ be finite abelian. There is a positive integer $\mathfrak f$ (the conductor) such that (1) a prime $p$ ramifies in $K$ iff $p\mid\mathfrak f$, and (2) $K\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{\mathfrak f})$.
Honest remark Part (2) is the Kronecker–Weber theorem, so the conductor theorem is equivalent to it. It follows from the conductor–discriminant formula and Artin reciprocity (class field theory). What we have proved elementarily is: cyclotomic fields are abelian with group $U_n$, all quadratic fields are cyclotomic, and the full theorem reduces to cyclic $p$-power extensions.
Theorem 3 Let $K/\mathbb{Q}$ be cyclic of degree $p^n$ with $p$ the only ramified prime. Then $K\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{p^{n+1}})$ for odd $p$, and $K\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{2^{n+2}})$ for $p=2$.
Question 12 Sketch the proof of Theorem 3.
Only $p$ ramifies, so the conductor $\mathfrak f$ is a power of $p$ (conductor theorem, part 1). The conductor–discriminant formula $\mathrm{Disc}(K)=\prod_\chi\mathfrak f(\chi)$ over the characters of $\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})$ together with the discriminant of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{p^{n+1}})$ shows $\mathfrak f\mid p^{n+1}$ ($p$ odd), respectively $\mathfrak f\mid 2^{n+2}$. Then (2) gives $K\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{\mathfrak f})\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{p^{n+1}})$. (This is a sketch relying on class field theory.)

§7 Assembling the proof

Question 13 Assemble the Kronecker–Weber theorem from the pieces.
Step 1. By the Proposition (§5) it suffices to treat cyclic $K/\mathbb{Q}$ of degree $p^n$.
Step 2. For such $K$ the conductor theorem gives $K\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{\mathfrak f})$.
Step 3. A general finite abelian $K$ is the compositum $K_1\cdots K_m$ of such fields, $K_j\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n_j})$, hence $K\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_N)$, $N=\operatorname{lcm}(n_j)$. ∎
Status: Step 2 uses class field theory; an elementary proof would prove the conductor statement for $p$-power cyclic fields directly from the structure of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{p^n})$ and Gauss sums.
Consequences (1) $\mathbb{Q}^{\mathrm{ab}}=\bigcup_n\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$. (2) $\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}^{\mathrm{ab}}/\mathbb{Q})\cong\varprojlim U_n=\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\,*}=\prod_p\mathbb{Z}_p^*$, the unit group of the profinite integers.
Kronecker–Weber is the first case of class field theory: abelian extensions of a number field are governed by its idèle class group. For imaginary quadratic fields the analogue uses complex multiplication of elliptic curves; for general number fields this is Hilbert's 12th problem, still open.

§8 Exercises with solutions

Exercise 1$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_5)\supseteq\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt5)$.
Lemma 1 with $p=5\equiv1\pmod4$: $\tau=\zeta+\zeta^4-\zeta^2-\zeta^3$ satisfies $\tau^2=5$.
Exercise 2$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{|D|})$, $D$ the discriminant of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)$.
$D=d$ if $d\equiv1\pmod4$, so $d=\prod p_i^*$ and $\sqrt D=\prod\sqrt{p_i^*}\in\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{|d|})$. If $d\equiv 2,3\pmod 4$, $D=4d$: for odd $d$ use $\sqrt{4d}=2\sqrt d$ and $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{4|d|})$; for even $d$ use $|D|=4|d|=8|d'|$ and the case analysis of Lemma 2. (This is the quadratic case of the conductor theorem; the explorer in §4 gives $n=|D|$.)
Exercise 3For odd $p$, $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$ has a unique quadratic subfield, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{p^*})$.
$\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)/\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbb{Z}/(p-1)$ is cyclic of even order, so it has exactly one subgroup of index 2, hence exactly one quadratic subfield; by Lemma 1 it is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{p^*})$.
Exercise 4$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_8)\supseteq\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2),\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-2})$.
$\zeta_8+\zeta_8^{-1}=\sqrt2$ and $\zeta_8-\zeta_8^{-1}=i\sqrt2$, whose square is $-2$. Together with $i=\zeta_8^2$: $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_8)=\mathbb{Q}(i,\sqrt2)$ of degree $4=\varphi(8)$.
Exercise 5Conductor of the cyclic cubic field inside $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_9)$.
It lies in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_9)$ and is ramified only at 3, so the conductor is a power of 3 dividing 9. It is not inside $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_3)$ (degree 2, and $3\nmid2$), so the conductor is $9$.
Exercise 6For odd $p$, the degree-$p$ subfield of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{p^2})$ is the cyclic $p$-extension of conductor $p^2$.
$\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{p^2})/\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbb{Z}/(p-1)\times\mathbb{Z}/p$; the fixed field of the order-$(p-1)$ subgroup has degree $p$ and is cyclic. It is not in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$, whose degree $p-1$ is prime to $p$, so its conductor is not $p$; being a power of $p$ dividing $p^2$, it is $p^2$.

§10 SAQ — Short Answer Questions

Answer on paper first, then reveal.

SAQ 1State the Kronecker–Weber theorem.
Every finite abelian extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ lies in some $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$; $\mathbb{Q}^{\mathrm{ab}}=\bigcup\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$.
SAQ 2Degree of $\Phi_n$ and of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)/\mathbb{Q}$?
$\varphi(n)$ for both.
SAQ 3What is $\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)/\mathbb{Q})$?
$(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^*$ via $\sigma\mapsto k$ where $\sigma(\zeta_n)=\zeta_n^k$.
SAQ 4What is $\Phi_p(x)$ and $\Phi_{2m}(x)$ for odd $m$?
$\Phi_p=1+x+\dots+x^{p-1}$; $\Phi_{2m}(x)=\Phi_m(-x)$.
SAQ 5Which quadratic field lies in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$?
$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{p^*})$, $p^*=(-1)^{(p-1)/2}p$.
SAQ 6Why does it suffice to treat cyclic extensions of prime-power degree?
A finite abelian group is a product of cyclic $p$-groups, so $K$ is a compositum of cyclic prime-power subextensions, and a compositum of subfields of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n_j})$ lies in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{\mathrm{lcm}})$.
SAQ 7Is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]2)$ in a cyclotomic field?
No. It is not Galois over $\mathbb{Q}$ (its conjugate $\omega\sqrt[3]2$ is not real), so it is not abelian, hence not a subfield of any $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$.

§11 Notation

$\zeta_n=e^{2\pi i/n}$; $R_n$ roots of unity; $m_n$ primitive roots; $\Phi_n=\prod_{\alpha\in m_n}(x-\alpha)$; $x^n-1=\prod_{d\mid n}\Phi_d$; $U_n=(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^*$; $\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)/\mathbb{Q})\cong U_n$; $\mathbb{Q}^{\mathrm{ab}}=\bigcup_n\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$.
