Seven Lectures on Field Extensions

Lecture 3
Algebraic Elements: Worked Problems

Seven problems, each with a solution box — fields of algebraic elements, isomorphism, generators, separability.

§1 Algebraic elements form a field

Question 1 — algebraic elements form a field Let $\mathbb{F}$ be a field and $\mathbb{K}\supseteq\mathbb{F}$. Show that the set of elements of $\mathbb{K}$ algebraic over $\mathbb{F}$ is a field. (The matrix argument from your notebook page.)
Write down the $m\cdot n$ numbers $a^ib^j$. Multiplying any of them by $a$ gives an $\mathbb{F}$-combination of them. Read this as a matrix equation $DC=aC$.
Setup. Let $a,b$ satisfy $a_na^n+\dots+a_0=0$ and $b_mb^m+\dots+b_0=0$ with coefficients in $\mathbb{F}$, $a_n,b_m\ne0$.
Reduction. $-b$ is algebraic: it is a root of $(-1)^mb_mx^m+(-1)^{m-1}b_{m-1}x^{m-1}+\dots+b_0$. If $b\ne0$, then $1/b$ is a root of $b_m+b_{m-1}x+\dots+b_0x^m$ (divide the equation for $b$ by $b^m$). Hence it suffices to show $a+b$ and $ab$ are algebraic, since $a-b=a+(-b)$ and $a/b=a\cdot(1/b)$.
The table. From the equations, $a^n=-(a_{n-1}a^{n-1}+\dots+a_0)/a_n$ and $b^m=-(b_{m-1}b^{m-1}+\dots+b_0)/b_m$. Consider the $mn$ numbers $$\begin{array}{cccc}1&a&\cdots&a^{n-1}\\ b&ab&\cdots&a^{n-1}b\\ \vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\ b^{m-1}&ab^{m-1}&\cdots&a^{n-1}b^{m-1}\end{array}$$ listed in any order as $c_1,\dots,c_R$, $R=mn$ (take $c_1=1$). For each $i$, $a\,c_i$ is an $\mathbb{F}$-linear combination of the $c_j$: if $c_i$ is not in the last column, $ac_i$ is the entry to its right; if it is in the last column, $a\cdot a^{n-1}b^k=a^nb^k$ and $a^n$ is replaced by lower powers of $a$. So $ac_i=\sum_jd_{ij}c_j$ with $d_{ij}\in\mathbb{F}$.
Matrices. With $C=(c_1,\dots,c_R)^T\ne0$ and $D=(d_{ij})$, we get $DC=aC$, i.e. $(D-aI)C=0$ with $C\ne0$, so $\det(D-aI)=0$: $a$ is a root of the characteristic polynomial $\det(D-\lambda I)\in\mathbb{F}[\lambda]$, which is non-zero. Similarly there is $E$ over $\mathbb{F}$ with $bC=EC$.
Sum. $(D+E)C=aC+bC=(a+b)C$, so $a+b$ is a root of $\det(D+E-\lambda I)\in\mathbb{F}[\lambda]$.
Product. $(ED)C=E(DC)=E(aC)=a(EC)=a(bC)=abC$ (scalars $a$ commute with matrices over $\mathbb{F}$ acting on $C$ entrywise), so $ab$ is a root of $\det(ED-\lambda I)\in\mathbb{F}[\lambda]$. Both polynomials are monic up to sign, hence non-zero. ∎
Compare: the proof in Lecture 2 §5 uses finiteness of $\mathbb{F}(a,b)$ instead; this one gives an explicit polynomial.

§2 Isomorphism, degree and generators

Question 2 Show that $\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt2]$ and $\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt3]$ are not isomorphic.
An isomorphism must fix $\mathbb{Q}$. Where can $\sqrt2$ go?
Suppose $\phi:\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt2]\to\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt3]$ is a ring isomorphism. Then $\phi(1)=1$, $\phi(2)=\phi(1+1)=2$, and similarly $\phi(n)=n$, $\phi(1/n)=1/n$, so $\phi$ fixes $\mathbb{Q}$. Write $\phi(\sqrt2)=a+b\sqrt3$ ($a,b\in\mathbb{Q}$). Then $2=\phi(2)=\phi(\sqrt2)^2=a^2+3b^2+2ab\sqrt3$, so $ab=0$ and $a^2+3b^2=2$. If $b=0$: $a^2=2$, impossible for rational $a$. If $a=0$: $3b^2=2$, so $(3b)^2=6$, impossible. Contradiction. ∎
(Equivalent view: $x^2-2$ has a root in $\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt2]$ but none in $\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt3]$.)
Question 3 Let $\mathbb{E}$ be an extension of $\mathbb{F}$. Show $[\mathbb{E}:\mathbb{F}]=1$ if and only if $\mathbb{E}=\mathbb{F}$.
(⇐) $\mathbb{F}$ over itself has basis $\{1\}$: degree 1. (⇒) The element $1\ne0$ is linearly independent, and the dimension is $1$, so $\{1\}$ is a basis of $\mathbb{E}$ over $\mathbb{F}$. Hence every $e\in\mathbb{E}$ is $e=\lambda\cdot1=\lambda\in\mathbb{F}$, i.e. $\mathbb{E}\subseteq\mathbb{F}$, and $\mathbb{E}=\mathbb{F}$. ∎
Consequence used below: if $\mathbb{F}\subseteq\mathbb{L}\subseteq\mathbb{E}$ and $[\mathbb{E}:\mathbb{L}]=1$ then $\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{E}$.
Question 4 If $[\mathbb{F}[\alpha]:\mathbb{F}]=3$, show $\mathbb{F}[\alpha]=\mathbb{F}[\alpha^2]$.
Tower $\mathbb{F}\subseteq\mathbb{F}[\alpha^2]\subseteq\mathbb{F}[\alpha]$. What degrees are possible for $[\mathbb{F}[\alpha^2]:\mathbb{F}]$?
Clearly $\mathbb{F}\subseteq\mathbb{F}[\alpha^2]\subseteq\mathbb{F}[\alpha]$. By the Tower Law $3=[\mathbb{F}[\alpha]:\mathbb{F}[\alpha^2]]\cdot[\mathbb{F}[\alpha^2]:\mathbb{F}]$, so $[\mathbb{F}[\alpha^2]:\mathbb{F}]\in\{1,3\}$. If it were $1$, then $\alpha^2=c\in\mathbb{F}$ (Question 3), so $\alpha$ is a root of $x^2-c$, and its minimal polynomial has degree $\le2$, contradicting degree $3$. So $[\mathbb{F}[\alpha^2]:\mathbb{F}]=3$ and $[\mathbb{F}[\alpha]:\mathbb{F}[\alpha^2]]=1$, giving $\mathbb{F}[\alpha]=\mathbb{F}[\alpha^2]$ by Question 3. ∎
Question 5 Show $\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt2,\sqrt3]=\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt2+\sqrt3]$.
Put $\gamma=\sqrt2+\sqrt3$. Compute $\gamma^2$ and $\gamma\sqrt2$.
($\supseteq$) $\gamma\in\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt2,\sqrt3]$. ($\subseteq$) $\gamma^2=5+2\sqrt6$, so $\sqrt6=(\gamma^2-5)/2\in\mathbb{Q}[\gamma]$. Also $\gamma\sqrt2=2+\sqrt6$, so $\sqrt2=(2+\sqrt6)/\gamma\in\mathbb{Q}[\gamma]$ ($\mathbb{Q}[\gamma]$ is a field since $\gamma$ is algebraic). Then $\sqrt3=\gamma-\sqrt2\in\mathbb{Q}[\gamma]$. Hence equality. ∎
Moreover $(\gamma^2-5)^2=24$ gives $\gamma^4-10\gamma^2+1=0$; since $[\mathbb{Q}[\gamma]:\mathbb{Q}]=4$ (Lecture 2, Question 6), this is the minimal polynomial of $\gamma$. So the field with basis $\{1,\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt6\}$ is a simple extension.

§3 $\mathbb{F}(\alpha)$ — the smallest field containing $\mathbb{F}$ and $\alpha$

Question 6 Let $\mathbb{K}\supseteq\mathbb{F}$ and $\alpha\in\mathbb{K}$. Show that $\mathbb{F}(\alpha)$, the field of fractions of $\mathbb{F}[\alpha]$, is the smallest subfield of $\mathbb{K}$ containing $\mathbb{F}$ and $\alpha$.
$\mathbb{F}[\alpha]=\{f(\alpha):f\in\mathbb{F}[x]\}$ is a subring of the field $\mathbb{K}$, hence an integral domain. Its field of fractions embeds in $\mathbb{K}$ as $L=\{f(\alpha)g(\alpha)^{-1}:f,g\in\mathbb{F}[x],\ g(\alpha)\ne0\}$, which is closed under $+,\times$, inverses of non-zero elements, and contains $\mathbb{F}$ and $\alpha$: so $L$ is a subfield of $\mathbb{K}$ containing $\mathbb{F}$ and $\alpha$. Smallest: if $M$ is any subfield containing $\mathbb{F}$ and $\alpha$, it is closed under sums and products, so $\mathbb{F}[\alpha]\subseteq M$; being a field, $M$ also contains all quotients $f(\alpha)/g(\alpha)$, so $L\subseteq M$. Thus $L$ is the intersection of all such subfields, which is exactly $\mathbb{F}(\alpha)$. ∎
If $\alpha$ is algebraic, $\mathbb{F}[\alpha]$ is already a field, so $\mathbb{F}(\alpha)=\mathbb{F}[\alpha]$; if $\alpha$ is transcendental, $\mathbb{F}(\alpha)\cong\mathbb{F}(x)$.

§4 Separability and the primitive element theorem

Definitions — separability
  1. A non-zero $f\in\mathbb{F}[x]$ is separable if it has no repeated roots in a splitting field; equivalently $\gcd(f,f')=1$. (Some books call $f$ separable when each irreducible factor is; for irreducible $f$ the two definitions agree.)
  2. An algebraic element $\alpha\in\mathbb{K}$ is separable over $\mathbb{F}$ if its minimal polynomial is separable.
  3. An algebraic extension $\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F}$ is separable if every element of $\mathbb{K}$ is separable over $\mathbb{F}$.
In characteristic $0$ every irreducible polynomial is separable, and so are all algebraic extensions. An irreducible $f$ is inseparable iff $f'=0$ (possible only in characteristic $p$, i.e. $f(x)=g(x^p)$); example: $x^p-t$ over $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$. Finite fields have only separable extensions.
Question 7 — Primitive element theorem Show: every finite separable extension $\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F}$ is simple, i.e. $\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{F}(\gamma)$ for some $\gamma$.
Finite field: $\mathbb{K}^\times$ is cyclic. Infinite field: reduce to two generators $\alpha,\beta$ and take $\gamma=\alpha+c\beta$ with $c\in\mathbb{F}$ chosen to avoid finitely many bad values.
Case 1: $\mathbb{F}$ finite. Then $\mathbb{K}$ is finite and $\mathbb{K}^\times$ is cyclic, generated by some $\gamma$; so $\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{F}(\gamma)$.
Case 2: $\mathbb{F}$ infinite. $\mathbb{K}$ is finite over $\mathbb{F}$, so $\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{F}(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ with all $\alpha_i$ separable. By induction (using $\mathbb{F}(a,b)=\mathbb{F}(a)(b)$) it suffices to prove $\mathbb{F}(\alpha,\beta)=\mathbb{F}(\gamma)$ when $\beta$ is separable.
Let $f,g$ be the minimal polynomials of $\alpha,\beta$, and in a splitting field let $\alpha=\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_r$ and $\beta=\beta_1,\dots,\beta_s$ be their roots; the $\beta_j$ are distinct since $g$ is separable. For each $i$ and each $j\ne1$ the equation $\alpha_i+c\beta_j=\alpha_1+c\beta_1$ has at most one solution $c$. As $\mathbb{F}$ is infinite, choose $c\in\mathbb{F}$ avoiding all these finitely many values, and put $\gamma=\alpha+c\beta$.
Let $h(x)=f(\gamma-cx)\in\mathbb{F}(\gamma)[x]$. Then $h(\beta)=f(\alpha)=0$ and $g(\beta)=0$. If $\beta_j$ ($j\ne1$) were a root of $h$, then $\gamma-c\beta_j=\alpha_i$ for some $i$, i.e. $\alpha_i+c\beta_j=\alpha+c\beta$, excluded by the choice of $c$. So the only common root of $g$ and $h$ is $\beta$, and it is simple in $g$: $\gcd(g,h)=x-\beta$. A gcd does not change under field extension, so $x-\beta\in\mathbb{F}(\gamma)[x]$, i.e. $\beta\in\mathbb{F}(\gamma)$, and then $\alpha=\gamma-c\beta\in\mathbb{F}(\gamma)$. Hence $\mathbb{F}(\alpha,\beta)=\mathbb{F}(\gamma)$. ∎
Example: $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2+\sqrt3)$ (Question 5), where $c=1$ works.

§6 SAQ — Short Answer Questions

Answer on paper first, then reveal.

SAQ 1Why does a ring isomorphism $\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt2]\to\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt3]$ have to fix $\mathbb{Q}$?
It sends $1\mapsto1$, hence $n\mapsto n$ and $1/n\mapsto1/n$.
SAQ 2When is $\mathbb{F}(\alpha)=\mathbb{F}[\alpha]$?
Exactly when $\alpha$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{F}$.
SAQ 3Define a separable polynomial.
A polynomial with no repeated root in a splitting field, equivalently $\gcd(f,f')=1$.
SAQ 4Why is every irreducible polynomial over $\mathbb{Q}$ separable?
If $f$ is irreducible, $\gcd(f,f')$ divides $f$, so is $1$ or $f$; but $f'\ne0$ has smaller degree in characteristic $0$, so it is $1$.
SAQ 5State the primitive element theorem.
Every finite separable extension $\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F}$ is simple: $\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{F}(\gamma)$ for some $\gamma$.
SAQ 6Give an inseparable irreducible polynomial.
$x^p-t$ over $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$: its derivative is $0$ and $x^p-t=(x-\sqrt[p]t)^p$ in a splitting field.
SAQ 7What is the degree of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, and a basis?
$4$, with basis $\{1,\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt6\}$.
