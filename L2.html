Seven Lectures on Field Extensions

Lecture 2
Adjoining Roots & the Minimal Polynomial

How to build a field in which a polynomial finally has a root.

§1 Adjoining a root

Theorem 1 (Kronecker) Let $f(x)\in\mathbb{F}[x]$ be irreducible. Then there is a field $\mathbb{K}$ containing an isomorphic copy of $\mathbb{F}$ in which $f$ has a root.
Question 1 Prove Theorem 1.
Take $\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{F}[x]/\langle f\rangle$ and the root $\alpha=x+\langle f\rangle$.
Put $I=\langle f\rangle$, $\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{F}[x]/I$. $\mathbb{F}[x]$ is a Euclidean domain (so a PID), $f$ is irreducible, hence $I$ is maximal and $\mathbb{K}$ is a field.
Copy of $\mathbb{F}$. $\phi:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{K}$, $\phi(a)=a+I$ is a ring homomorphism, injective (a nonzero constant is not a multiple of $f$), so $\mathbb{F}\cong\phi(\mathbb{F})\subseteq\mathbb{K}$.
Root. Let $\alpha=x+I$ and $f=a_0+\dots+a_nx^n$. Then $f(\alpha)=(a_0+I)+(a_1+I)(x+I)+\dots=a_0+a_1x+\dots+a_nx^n+I=f+I=I=0_{\mathbb{K}}$. ∎

Question 4 Is $\mathbb{Q}[x]/\langle x^2-1\rangle$ a field? What goes wrong?
No. $x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1)$ is reducible, so $\langle x^2-1\rangle$ is not maximal. $(x-1)(x+1)=0$ in the quotient with both factors nonzero: zero divisors.

§2 Generated extensions and simple extensions

Definition For $a_1,\dots,a_n\in\mathbb{K}\supseteq\mathbb{F}$, the intersection of all subfields of $\mathbb{K}$ containing $\mathbb{F}$ and the $a_i$ is the smallest such field, written $\mathbb{F}(a_1,\dots,a_n)$. It is a finitely generated extension of $\mathbb{F}$. When $n=1$, $\mathbb{F}(a)$ is a simple extension.
Algebraic or transcendental via degree $a\in\mathbb{K}$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{F}$ if $[\mathbb{F}(a):\mathbb{F}]<\infty$ and transcendental if $[\mathbb{F}(a):\mathbb{F}]=\infty$. For $a\in\mathbb{F}$: $\mathbb{F}(a)=\mathbb{F}$, degree 1.
Question 5 Show $\mathbb{F}(a,b)=\mathbb{F}(a)(b)$.
$\mathbb{F}(a)(b)$ is a field containing $\mathbb{F},a,b$, so $\mathbb{F}(a,b)\subseteq\mathbb{F}(a)(b)$. Conversely $\mathbb{F}(a,b)$ is a field containing $\mathbb{F}$ and $a$, hence $\mathbb{F}(a)$, and it contains $b$, so $\mathbb{F}(a)(b)\subseteq\mathbb{F}(a,b)$. This gives the tower $\mathbb{F}\subseteq\mathbb{F}(a_1)\subseteq\mathbb{F}(a_1,a_2)\subseteq\dots$
Question 6 Describe $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)$ as a tower and give its degree (answer: 4).
$\mathbb{Q}\subset\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)\subset\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)(\sqrt3)$. $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2):\mathbb{Q}]=2$. $\sqrt3\notin\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$ (else $\sqrt3=a+b\sqrt2$ gives $3=a^2+2b^2+2ab\sqrt2$, forcing $ab=0$, impossible), so $x^2-3$ stays irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$: degree 2. Tower Law: $2\cdot2=4$.
Lattice diagram for Question 6 — subfields of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)$
Every edge has degree $2$, and by the Tower Law the top has degree $4$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. The extension is Galois with group $\{e,\sigma,\tau,\sigma\tau\}\cong\mathbb{Z}_2\times\mathbb{Z}_2$, which has exactly three subgroups of order $2$; by the Galois correspondence there are exactly three intermediate fields, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2),\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt3),\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt6)$ (the fixed fields of $\tau$, $\sigma$, $\sigma\tau$). Subgroups go down as fields go up. Since $4$ is not prime, Theorem 2 of Lecture 1 allows intermediate fields here, unlike the prime-degree case in Lecture 1, Question 6.

§3 A closer look at $\mathbb{F}(a)$

Define $\psi:\mathbb{F}[x]\to\mathbb{F}[a]$, $\psi(f)=f(a)$. It is an onto ring homomorphism, and $\ker\psi=\{f:f(a)=0\}$ is an ideal of $\mathbb{F}[x]$.

Flowchart — what $\ker\psi$ tells us
Question 7 If $a$ is transcendental over $\mathbb{F}$, why is $\mathbb{F}[a]$ not a field, and why is $[\mathbb{F}(a):\mathbb{F}]=\infty$?
Here $\mathbb{F}[a]\cong\mathbb{F}[x]$, whose only units are the non-zero constants. So $a$ (non-constant) has no inverse in $\mathbb{F}[a]$. The elements $1,a,a^2,\dots$ are independent over $\mathbb{F}$, so the dimension is infinite. Example: $\mathbb{Q}[\pi]\cong\mathbb{Q}[x]$ and $\mathbb{Q}(\pi)\cong\mathbb{Q}(x)$.
Question 8 If $a$ and $b$ are both transcendental over $\mathbb{F}$, is $\mathbb{F}[a]\cong\mathbb{F}[b]$?
Yes: $\mathbb{F}[a]\cong\mathbb{F}[x]\cong\mathbb{F}[b]$, via $f(a)\mapsto f(b)$. So $\mathbb{Q}(\pi)\cong\mathbb{Q}(e)$ as abstract fields, though $\pi\ne e$.

§4 The minimal polynomial

Definition If $\alpha$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{F}$, its minimal polynomial is the monic $q(x)\in\mathbb{F}[x]$ of least degree with $q(\alpha)=0$. $\alpha$ has degree $n$ over $\mathbb{F}$ if $\deg q=n$.
Question 9 Prove: $q$ is irreducible, and if $f(\alpha)=0$ then $q\mid f$.
Use the division algorithm and minimality of $\deg q$.
Divides. Write $f=qs+r$ with $r=0$ or $\deg r<\deg q$. Then $r(\alpha)=f(\alpha)-q(\alpha)s(\alpha)=0$; minimality forces $r=0$.
Irreducible. If $q=gh$ with lower degrees, then $g(\alpha)h(\alpha)=0$ in a field, so $g(\alpha)=0$ or $h(\alpha)=0$, contradicting minimality. ∎ So $\ker\psi=\langle q\rangle$ and the minimal polynomial is unique.
Question 10 — find them Minimal polynomial of $2-i$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ and over $\mathbb{C}$; and of $1+i$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{C}$.
$2-i$: $(x-2)^2=-1$, so $x^2-4x+5$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ (irreducible: no rational root), and $x-2+i$ over $\mathbb{C}$. Similarly $1+i$: $x^2-2x+2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, and $x-1-i$ over $\mathbb{C}$. The minimal polynomial depends on the base field!
Theorem 2 Let $\alpha$ be algebraic of degree $n$ over $\mathbb{F}$. Then (1) $\mathbb{F}(\alpha)=\mathbb{F}[\alpha]$, and (2) $\mathbb{F}(\alpha)=\{a_0+a_1\alpha+\dots+a_{n-1}\alpha^{n-1}:a_i\in\mathbb{F}\}$; so $[\mathbb{F}(\alpha):\mathbb{F}]=n$ and $\mathbb{F}(\alpha)\cong\mathbb{F}[x]/\langle q\rangle$.

§5 Finitely generated, algebraic elements, and towers

Theorem 3 $\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F}$ is finite iff $\mathbb{K}$ is generated by finitely many algebraic elements. If $a_1,\dots,a_k$ are algebraic of degrees $n_1,\dots,n_k$, then $[\mathbb{F}(a_1,\dots,a_k):\mathbb{F}]\le n_1\cdots n_k$.
Question 13 Prove Theorem 3.
($\Rightarrow$) A finite basis generates $\mathbb{K}$ over $\mathbb{F}$, and each element is algebraic (finite ⇒ algebraic).
($\Leftarrow$) $\mathbb{F}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_1=\mathbb{F}(a_1)\subseteq\dots\subseteq\mathbb{F}_k$ with $\mathbb{F}_i=\mathbb{F}_{i-1}(a_i)$. $a_i$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{F}$, hence over $\mathbb{F}_{i-1}$ with minimal polynomial dividing the old one, so $[\mathbb{F}_i:\mathbb{F}_{i-1}]\le n_i$. Tower Law: $[\mathbb{F}_k:\mathbb{F}]\le n_1\cdots n_k$. ∎
Corollary If $a,b\in\mathbb{K}$ are algebraic over $\mathbb{F}$, then $\mathbb{F}(a,b)$ is finite over $\mathbb{F}$; hence $a\pm b$, $ab$, $a/b$ ($b\ne0$) are algebraic. The algebraic elements of $\mathbb{K}$ over $\mathbb{F}$ form a subfield.
Question 14 Now justify the examples from Lecture 1: $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt5,\sqrt7,\dots)$ and $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2,\sqrt[3]2,\sqrt[4]2,\dots)$ are algebraic but not finite extensions of $\mathbb{Q}$.
Every element is a rational function in finitely many generators, so lies in a finitely generated sub-extension, which is finite; by the Corollary the element is algebraic. Not finite: the second field contains $\sqrt[n]2$ of degree $n$ for every $n$, and if $[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{Q}]=N$ were finite, Theorem 2 of Lecture 1 would give $n\mid N$ for every $n$, impossible. (The first field is similar: it contains $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{p_1},\dots,\sqrt{p_k})$ of degree $2^k$ for every $k$.)
Theorem 4 (Tower of algebraic extensions) If $\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F}$ and $\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}$ are algebraic, then $\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{F}$ is algebraic.
Question 15 Prove Theorem 4.
Take $c\in\mathbb{L}$ and its minimal polynomial over $\mathbb{K}$. Its coefficients live in $\mathbb{K}$ — adjoin them to $\mathbb{F}$ first.
Let $c\in\mathbb{L}$ with minimal polynomial $x^m+k_{m-1}x^{m-1}+\dots+k_0$ over $\mathbb{K}$. Put $\mathbb{F}_0=\mathbb{F}(k_0,\dots,k_{m-1})$. Each $k_i$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{F}$, so $[\mathbb{F}_0:\mathbb{F}]<\infty$ (Theorem 3). Since $c$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{F}_0$, $[\mathbb{F}_0(c):\mathbb{F}_0]<\infty$. By the Tower Law $[\mathbb{F}_0(c):\mathbb{F}]<\infty$, so $c$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{F}$. ∎

§6 Compositum

Definition For subfields $\mathbb{K}_1,\mathbb{K}_2$ of $\mathbb{K}$, the composite $\mathbb{K}_1\mathbb{K}_2$ is the smallest subfield of $\mathbb{K}$ containing both (similarly for any collection of subfields).
Question 16 Identify $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)\,\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt3)$ and give its degree over $\mathbb{Q}$.
It contains $\mathbb{Q},\sqrt2,\sqrt3$ and is generated by them, so it is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)$, of degree 4 (Question 6).

§8 SAQ — Short Answer Questions

Answer on paper first, then reveal.

SAQ 1State Kronecker's theorem (adjoining a root).
For irreducible $f\in\mathbb{F}[x]$, $\mathbb{F}[x]/\langle f\rangle$ is a field containing a copy of $\mathbb{F}$ and the root $x+\langle f\rangle$ of $f$.
SAQ 2What is the unique form of an element of $\mathbb{F}[x]/\langle f\rangle$, $\deg f=n$?
$c_0+c_1\alpha+\dots+c_{n-1}\alpha^{n-1}$, $c_i\in\mathbb{F}$.
SAQ 3Define a simple extension.
An extension of the form $\mathbb{F}(a)$, generated by a single element.
SAQ 4When is $\mathbb{F}[a]=\mathbb{F}(a)$?
Exactly when $a$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{F}$.
SAQ 5Why is the minimal polynomial irreducible?
A factorisation $q=gh$ would give $g(\alpha)h(\alpha)=0$, so one factor vanishes at $\alpha$ with smaller degree.
SAQ 6Bound $[\mathbb{F}(a,b):\mathbb{F}]$ for algebraic $a,b$ of degrees $m,n$.
At most $mn$.
SAQ 7Define the composite of two subfields.
The smallest subfield containing both.
