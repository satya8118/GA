Burnside’s paqb Theorem

Every group of order $p^aq^b$ is solvable. The heart of the matter is showing that no non-abelian simple group has such an order. The proof mixes elementary group theory with a surprisingly small amount of character theory. Proofs are collapsed — try to guess them first.

The big picture

Theorem (Burnside)Let $G$ be a group of order $p^{a}q^{b}$, where $p,q$ are primes and $a,b\ge 0$. Then $G$ is solvable. Equivalently, $G$ is not simple unless it is cyclic of prime order (or trivial).

Solvability follows from non-simplicity by induction on $|G|$: a minimal counterexample has a non-trivial proper normal subgroup $N$, and both $N$ and $G/N$ are solvable by minimality, so $G$ is too. The real work is therefore the non-simplicity statement, which is what we prove below.

Before you startYou should be comfortable with: simple (only normal subgroups are $\{e\}$ and $G$); the conjugacy class $\mathrm{cl}(x)=\{gxg^{-1}\}$ and centralizer $C_G(x)$; orbit–stabilizer, so $|\mathrm{cl}(x)|=[G:C_G(x)]$; Sylow’s theorem; and basic character facts ($\chi(e)=\dim$, characters are class functions, orthogonality). An algebraic integer is a root of a monic integer polynomial; sums and products of these are again algebraic integers, and a rational one is an ordinary integer.
Strategy in one sentenceFind a non-trivial class whose size is a power of a single prime (easy, using a Sylow subgroup), then show that such a class forces a non-trivial normal subgroup (hard, using characters).

1 · Easy reductions

Lemma 1Every finite abelian simple group is cyclic of prime order.
ProofLet $g\ne e$. In an abelian group every subgroup is normal, so simplicity forces $\langle g\rangle=G$. If $|G|$ were composite, a proper non-trivial cyclic subgroup would exist, contradicting simplicity. ∎

So we may assume $G$ is non-abelian and simple. If $b=0$, then $G$ is a $p$-group, and $p$-groups have non-trivial center, so $G$ is not simple. Hence $a,b\ge1$.

Check yourself

Why is a $p$-group (non-abelian) never simple?

Reveal answer
$p$-groups have non-trivial center $Z(G)$, which is a normal subgroup. Since $G$ is non-abelian, $Z(G)\ne G$, so it is a proper non-trivial normal subgroup.

2 · Burnside’s theorem from the key proposition

Proposition (Main)Let $G$ be a finite non-abelian group with a conjugacy class $\mathcal C\ne\{e\}$ of size $p^t$ ($p$ prime, $t\ge0$). Then $G$ is not simple.

The case $t=0$ is easy: $\mathcal C=\{g\}$ means $g\in Z(G)$, so the center is non-trivial. We prove the proposition in Section 4; first see how it gives the theorem.

Intuition

Look for an element whose conjugacy class has size a power of one prime. Orbit–stabiliser says class size = index of the centraliser. If the centraliser contains a whole Sylow $q$-subgroup, the index has no $q$ in it — it is a power of $p$.

Proof of Burnside’s TheoremAssume $a,b\ge1$ and $G$ non-abelian simple. Let $Q$ be a Sylow $q$-subgroup, $|Q|=q^b$, and pick $x\ne e$ in $Z(Q)$ (non-trivial since $Q$ is a $q$-group). The centraliser $$C_G(x)=\{g\in G: gx=xg\}$$ contains $Q$, so $$[G:C_G(x)]=\frac{p^aq^b}{|C_G(x)|}$$ is a power of $p$. By orbit–stabiliser ($G$ acts on itself by conjugation) this index is the size of the class of $x$. Also $x\notin Z(G)$ since $Z(G)=\{e\}$ in a non-abelian simple group, so the class is non-trivial. The Main Proposition finishes. ∎
Example: run the proof on S₃ and A₄S₃ ($p=3,q=2$): $Q=\langle(12)\rangle$, so take $x=(12)$. Only $Q$ commutes with $x$, so $C_G(x)=Q$ and the index is $6/2=3=3^1$, the size of the class of transpositions. A₄ ($|A_4|=2^2\cdot 3$): take $Q=\langle(123)\rangle$ and $x=(123)$. Then $C_G(x)=Q$, index $12/3=4=2^2$, and indeed the 3-cycles split into classes of size 4. Neither group is simple ($A_3\trianglelefteq S_3$, $V_4\trianglelefteq A_4$), as the Main Proposition predicts.
Check yourself

Where did we use that $x\in Z(Q)$?

Reveal answer
It guarantees $Q\le C_G(x)$, so $q^b$ divides $|C_G(x)|$ and the index $[G:C_G(x)]$ is a power of $p$ only.
Example: S₃, order 2·3Conjugacy classes of $S_3$ — click an element to see its class. Sizes $1,3,2$ sum to $6$: the class equation. The class of transpositions has size $3=3^1$, a prime power!

RecapLemma 1 and the $p$-group case reduce us to a non-abelian simple $G$ with $a,b\ge1$. A Sylow subgroup and its center give an element $x$ whose class has size $p^t$. It remains to show that such a class forces a normal subgroup.

3 · The representation-theory toolkit

Everything here is over $\mathbb C$ and $G$ is finite. We introduce only what the main proof uses.

3.1 Representations

Definition (representation)A representation of $G$ is a group homomorphism $$\rho:G\longrightarrow GL(V),\qquad \rho(gh)=\rho(g)\rho(h),\quad \rho(e)=I,$$ where $V$ is a finite-dimensional complex vector space. The degree of $\rho$ is $d=\dim V$. It is irreducible if $V$ has no proper non-zero subspace preserved by every $\rho(g)$. Two representations are equivalent if they differ by a change of basis.
Examples (S₃)The trivial representation sends every $g$ to $1\in GL_1(\mathbb C)$. The sign representation sends $g$ to $\pm1$ according to parity. The standard representation of degree 2 lets $S_3$ act on the symmetries of an equilateral triangle: 3-cycles act as $120^\circ$ rotations and transpositions as reflections. These are all of the irreducibles of $S_3$.
Cited results(Maschke) Every representation of a finite group is a direct sum of irreducible representations.
(Number of irreducibles) For a finite group $G$, $$\#\{\text{irreducible representations of }G\text{ up to equivalence}\}=\#\{\text{conjugacy classes of }G\}.$$ We call this common number $s$.
Example$S_3$ has three conjugacy classes ($\{e\}$, the transpositions, the 3-cycles), hence exactly three irreducibles: trivial, sign, standard. $A_5$ has five classes (sizes $1,12,12,15,20$), hence five irreducibles, of degrees $1,3,3,4,5$.
Check yourself

An abelian group of order $n$ has how many irreducible representations, and of what degree?

Reveal answer
Every element is its own conjugacy class, so there are $n$ irreducibles. Since $n=\sum d_i^2$ (Proposition 3 below), all have degree $1$.

3.2 Characters

Definition (character)The character of $\rho$ is the function $$\chi_\rho:G\longrightarrow\mathbb C,\qquad \chi_\rho(g)=\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho(g).$$
Basic properties
  1. $\chi_\rho(e)=\dim V=d$.
  2. $\chi_\rho$ is a class function: $$\chi_\rho(xgx^{-1})=\chi_\rho(g)\quad\text{for all }x,g\in G.$$
  3. $\chi_\rho(g)$ is the sum of the $d$ eigenvalues of $\rho(g)$, and each eigenvalue is a root of unity. Hence $\chi_\rho(g)$ is an algebraic integer.
  4. Characters add over direct sums.
  5. The irreducible characters $\chi_1,\dots,\chi_s$ are orthonormal under $$\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\alpha(g)\overline{\beta(g)},$$ so the multiplicity of $\chi_i$ in any character $\chi$ is $\langle\chi,\chi_i\rangle$.
Proof of (i)–(iii)(i) $\rho(e)=I_d$ has trace $d$. (ii) $\mathrm{Tr}(\rho(x)\rho(g)\rho(x)^{-1})=\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho(g)$ since trace is invariant under conjugation. (iii) With $n=|G|$ we have $\rho(g)^n=\rho(g^n)=I$, so the minimal polynomial of $\rho(g)$ divides $X^n-1$, which has distinct roots. Hence $\rho(g)$ is diagonalizable with eigenvalues $\omega_1,\dots,\omega_d$ that are roots of unity, and $\chi_\rho(g)=\omega_1+\cdots+\omega_d$. Roots of unity are algebraic integers, and so are sums of them. (iv) and (v) are standard and cited.
Check yourself

Why does property (iii) need $G$ to be finite?

Reveal answer
It uses $g^{|G|}=e$. In an infinite group, $\rho(g)$ could have eigenvalues that are not roots of unity.

3.3 The regular representation

Definition (regular representation)Let $V$ have a basis $\{e_h\}_{h\in G}$ indexed by the group elements. The (left) regular representation is $$\mathrm{reg}:G\longrightarrow GL(V),\qquad \mathrm{reg}(g)\,e_h=e_{gh}\quad(h\in G),$$ where $\mathrm{reg}(g)$ is extended linearly.
Why this is a homomorphism

For any basis vector, $$\mathrm{reg}(g)\mathrm{reg}(h)\,e_k=\mathrm{reg}(g)\,e_{hk}=e_{ghk}=\mathrm{reg}(gh)\,e_k,$$ so $\mathrm{reg}(g)\mathrm{reg}(h)=\mathrm{reg}(gh)$. Also $\mathrm{reg}(e)=I$. So each $\mathrm{reg}(g)$ is invertible, with inverse $\mathrm{reg}(g^{-1})$, and $\mathrm{reg}$ is a homomorphism into $GL(V)$. Each $\mathrm{reg}(g)$ is a permutation matrix: it just shuffles the basis vectors by left multiplication.

Example: $G=\mathbb Z/3=\{e,g,g^2\}$In the ordered basis $e_e,e_g,e_{g^2}$, $\mathrm{reg}(g)$ is the cyclic permutation matrix $$\mathrm{reg}(g)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}.$$ Every diagonal entry is $0$, so $\chi_{\mathrm{reg}}(g)=0$, while $\mathrm{reg}(e)=I_3$ has trace $3$.
Lemma 2$$\chi_{\mathrm{reg}}(g)=0\ \text{ for }g\ne e,\qquad \chi_{\mathrm{reg}}(e)=|G|.$$
Intuition

$\mathrm{reg}(g)$ shuffles the basis without fixing any vector when $g\ne e$, so its matrix has zeros on the diagonal.

ProofThe $(h,h)$ diagonal entry of $\mathrm{reg}(g)$ is $1$ if $e_{gh}=e_h$, i.e. if $g=e$, and $0$ otherwise. So for $g\ne e$ every diagonal entry is $0$ and the trace vanishes. For $g=e$ the matrix is $I_{|G|}$. ∎
Proposition 3Let $\varphi_1,\dots,\varphi_s$ be the irreducible representations with degrees $d_i$ and characters $\chi_i$. Then $$\chi_{\mathrm{reg}}=\sum_{i=1}^s d_i\,\chi_i.$$
Intuition

Each irreducible $\varphi_i$ appears in the regular representation as many times as its own degree. Reading degrees off at $e$ gives $|G|=\sum d_i^2$.

ProofBy property (v) the multiplicity of $\chi_i$ in $\chi_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is $$\langle\chi_{\mathrm{reg}},\chi_i\rangle=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\chi_{\mathrm{reg}}(g)\overline{\chi_i(g)}.$$ By Lemma 2 only $g=e$ contributes, giving $$\frac1{|G|}\cdot|G|\cdot\overline{\chi_i(e)}=d_i.\ ∎$$
Example: regular representation of S₃Here $$\chi_{\mathrm{reg}}=\chi_{\mathrm{triv}}+\chi_{\mathrm{sign}}+2\,\chi_{\mathrm{std}},\qquad 6=1^2+1^2+2^2.$$ Evaluating on a transposition gives $0=1-1+2\cdot0$, as the Lemma predicts.
Check yourself

Why does the order matter in the definition? What goes wrong if we set $\mathrm{reg}(g)e_h=e_{hg}$?

Reveal answer
Then $\mathrm{reg}(g)\mathrm{reg}(h)e_k=e_{khg}$, while $\mathrm{reg}(gh)e_k=e_{kgh}$. This is an anti-homomorphism for non-abelian $G$, so one would use $e_{hg^{-1}}$ instead.

3.4 The one cited result

Cited result (black box)Fact. Let $\mathcal C$ be a conjugacy class of size $m$ and $\rho$ an irreducible representation of degree $d$ with $\gcd(m,d)=1$. Then either $$\rho(g)=\lambda I\ \text{ for all }g\in\mathcal C\ (\text{some }\lambda\in\mathbb C^\times),\qquad\text{or}\qquad \chi_\rho(g)=0\ \text{ for all }g\in\mathcal C.$$
Why believe the Fact? (sketch, not needed later)Since $\gcd(m,d)=1$, write $1=am+bd$. Then $$\frac{\chi(g)}d=a\cdot\frac{m\,\chi(g)}d+b\,\chi(g).$$ A standard fact says $m\chi(g)/d$ is an algebraic integer, so $\chi(g)/d$ is one too. It is an average of $d$ roots of unity, so every Galois conjugate has absolute value $\le1$. If it is non-zero, its norm is a non-zero integer of absolute value $\le1$, so every conjugate has absolute value exactly $1$. That means all eigenvalues of $\rho(g)$ agree, so $\rho(g)$ is a scalar. Otherwise $\chi(g)=0$.
Check yourself

In S₃, the 3-cycles form a class of size $m=2$ and the standard representation has $d=2$. Why does the Fact say nothing here?

Reveal answer
$\gcd(2,2)\ne1$. Indeed, $\rho(123)$ is a rotation by $120^\circ$, not a scalar, and $\chi(123)=-1\ne0$, so neither alternative holds. Coprimality is essential.

Check yourself

Why are the $\chi_i(g)$ algebraic integers?

Reveal answer
$g^{|G|}=e$, so each eigenvalue of $\rho(g)$ is a root of unity; $\chi_i(g)$ is a sum of roots of unity, hence an algebraic integer.

4 · Proof of the Main Proposition

Intuition

Assume $G$ is simple, so every non-trivial irreducible representation is faithful, which kills scalar action. The Fact then forces $\chi_k(g)=0$ whenever $p\nmid d_k$. Plug into $\chi_{\mathrm{reg}}(g)=0$: you get $-1/p$ as an algebraic integer. Absurd!

Predict first

Suppose $G$ is non-abelian simple and $\varphi_k$ is a non-trivial irreducible representation. What is its kernel? What does that say about which elements can act as scalars?

Reveal answer
The kernel is normal and not all of $G$, so it is $\{e\}$ and $\varphi_k$ is injective. Scalars form a normal subgroup of $GL_{d_k}$, so the elements acting as scalars form a normal subgroup of $G$, which must be trivial or $G$. It cannot be $G$, so only $e$ acts as a scalar.

Proof

Step 1 (faithfulness). Let $G$ be non-abelian simple, so $Z(G)=\{e\}$. Number the irreducibles $\varphi_1,\dots,\varphi_s$ (recall $s$ is the number of conjugacy classes), with $\varphi_1$ trivial ($d_1=1$). For $k>1$, $\ker\varphi_k$ is a normal subgroup not equal to $G$, so $\ker\varphi_k=\{e\}$: each $\varphi_k$ is injective. In particular $d_k>1$ (otherwise $G$ embeds in the abelian group $\mathbb C^\times$).

Step 2 (no scalars). Let $$Z_k=\{x\in G:\varphi_k(x)=\lambda I\text{ for some }\lambda\in\mathbb C^\times\}=\varphi_k^{-1}\big(Z(GL_{d_k}\mathbb C)\big).$$ As the preimage of a normal subgroup, $Z_k\trianglelefteq G$. By simplicity $Z_k\in\{\{e\},G\}$, and $Z_k=G$ would make $G\cong\varphi_k(G)$ abelian. Hence $Z_k=\{e\}$: no non-trivial $g\in\mathcal C$ acts as a scalar.

Step 3 (vanishing). If $p\nmid d_k$ then $\gcd(|\mathcal C|,d_k)=\gcd(p^t,d_k)=1$. By the Fact, $\chi_k(g)=0$ for $g\in\mathcal C$, $k>1$.

Step 4 (contradiction). For $g\in\mathcal C$, $g\neq e$: $$0=\chi_{\mathrm{reg}}(g)=1+\sum_{i\ge2}d_i\chi_i(g)=1+p\sum_{p\mid d_i}\frac{d_i}{p}\chi_i(g).$$ So $-\tfrac1p$ is an integral combination of the $\chi_i(g)$, which are algebraic integers. Thus $-1/p$ is an algebraic integer; but the only rational algebraic integers are in $\mathbb Z$. Contradiction. ∎

Check yourself

Why is $Z_k$ a normal subgroup of $G$?

Reveal answer
$Z_k=\varphi_k^{-1}(Z(GL_{d_k}\mathbb C))$, and the center of $GL_{d_k}$ is normal. The preimage of a normal subgroup under a homomorphism is normal.
Check yourself

Where is the hypothesis $|\mathcal C|=p^t$ used?

Reveal answer
Only in Step 3: it makes $p\nmid d_k$ imply $\gcd(|\mathcal C|,d_k)=1$, which lets us apply the Fact.
Check yourself

Why can $-1/p$ not be an algebraic integer?

Reveal answer
A rational number that is a root of a monic integer polynomial must be an integer (rational root theorem), and $-1/p\notin\mathbb Z$.

5 · Why A₅ escapes (and must)

Example: A₅, order 60A₅ is simple and non-abelian, so it must have no non-trivial class of prime-power size. Its class sizes are
$1+15+20+12+12=60$. None of $15,20,12$ is a prime power. ✓
Check yourself

Why does Burnside’s theorem not contradict the existence of $A_5$?

Reveal answer
$|A_5|=60=2^2\cdot3\cdot5$ involves three primes, so the theorem does not apply — and indeed no class has prime-power size.

Try it yourself

Where does the argument break for a group of order $p^aq^br^c$, such as $A_5$?

Reveal answer
The index of the centralizer of a central element of a Sylow $r$-subgroup is only guaranteed to avoid $r$; it can still involve both $p$ and $q$, so the class size need not be a prime power. $A_5$ confirms this: its non-trivial class sizes are $12,12,15,20$.
