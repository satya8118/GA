An Informal Exploration

Algebraic Varieties

Not a lecture. Not a course. Just a taste of where familiar mathematics can lead — from circles to objects that live in higher dimensions.

Curves Surfaces Singularities Beyond

01 / Starting Point

Where We Begin: The Curves You Know

In school mathematics, you have already met equations like these:

$x + y = 1$ — a straight line

$x^2 + y^2 = 1$ — a unit circle

$y = x^2$ — a parabola

Each equation describes a shape — a set of points in the plane that satisfy it. Plot them, and there they are: clean, familiar curves. These are algebraic curves: sets of points where a polynomial equation holds. Nothing mysterious so far.

$x + y = 1$

$x^2 + y^2 = 1$

$y = x^2$

There is a compact notation for all of this. Move everything to one side of the equation, and call the whole set of solutions $V(f)$ — the zero locus of $f$:

$$V(f) \;=\; \{(x,y) : f(x,y) = 0\}$$

The line above is $V(x+y-1)$, the circle is $V(x^2+y^2-1)$, the parabola is $V(y-x^2)$. A hyperbola fits the same mould — $V(xy-1)$ — and so, more surprisingly, does a pair of crossing lines: $V(xy)$ splits as $V(x)\cup V(y)$, the two axes together, produced by one single equation.

$xy = 1$  (hyperbola)

$xy = 0$  (two lines at once)

02 / Going Higher

From Curves to Surfaces

Now add a third variable $z$. The equation $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1$ describes a sphere in three-dimensional space. The equation $x^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 1$ describes a hyperboloid — a surface that flares outward like a cooling tower.

These are algebraic surfaces. The pattern is the same as before: a polynomial equation defines a geometric shape. But now we are in three dimensions, and the shapes become more interesting.

Drag the wireframes below to rotate them.

$x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1$

$x^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 1$

Each equation you write down "costs" one dimension: a single equation in 3-space $\mathbb{R}^3$ generically leaves a 2-dimensional surface, exactly as one equation in the plane left a 1-dimensional curve. A double cone, $x^2+y^2=z^2$, is another such surface — and one worth remembering, because slicing it with a plane is precisely where the words "conic section" come from.

Impose two surface equations at once, and the dimension drops again — down to a curve threading through space. The twisted cubic, $V(y-x^2,\,z-x^3)$, is exactly this: the intersection of a parabolic cylinder and a cubic surface, traced out by $(t,t^2,t^3)$ as $t$ runs over the reals.

$x^2 + y^2 = z^2$  (double cone)

$(t, t^2, t^3)$  (twisted cubic)

03 / The Turning Point

What Happens When We Raise the Degree?

All the examples above involve polynomials of degree 1 or 2. What happens with degree 3? Degree 4?

Consider the elliptic curve:

$$y^2 = x^3 - x$$

Its shape is qualitatively different from circles and parabolas. It has a closed oval and a separate branch that extends to infinity. This curve encodes deep number-theoretic information — it played a central role in the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem.

Or consider the lemniscate — a figure-eight shape from a degree-4 equation:

$$(x^2 + y^2)^2 = x^2 - y^2$$

Higher degree does not just mean "a more complicated version of the same thing." New phenomena emerge. Topology changes. The shapes become richer and stranger.

$y^2 = x^3 - x$  (elliptic curve)

$(x^2+y^2)^2 = x^2 - y^2$  (lemniscate)

04 / A Detour Worth Taking

Why Coefficients Know So Much

Before pinning down what a variety is, it's worth seeing where the coefficients of a polynomial come from, because the same idea resurfaces everywhere in this subject. Let $\alpha,\beta$ be the two roots of $x^2+bx+c$. Vieta's formulas say $\alpha+\beta=-b$ and $\alpha\beta=c$ — the coefficients are built from the roots, but built in a very particular way: symmetrically. Swap $\alpha\leftrightarrow\beta$ and neither expression changes.

That single fact decides which quantities you can compute from $b,c$ alone, and which you cannot:

Expression in the rootsEffect of swapping $\alpha\leftrightarrow\beta$In terms of $b,c$
$\alpha+\beta$unchanged$-b$
$\alpha\beta$unchanged$c$
$\alpha^2+\beta^2$unchanged$b^2-2c$
$\alpha^2\beta+\alpha\beta^2$unchanged$-bc$
$\alpha-\beta$flips signonly $(\alpha-\beta)^2=b^2-4c$ survives — the discriminant
$\alpha^2\beta$ alonebecomes $\beta^2\alpha$ — a different numbernot expressible in $b,c$ at all

$b$ and $c$ are built from the roots symmetrically, so they genuinely cannot tell $\alpha$ apart from $\beta$ — no formula in $b,c$ alone can single out "$\alpha^2\beta$" rather than "$\beta^2\alpha$." The same split shows up one root richer. Let $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ be the roots of $x^3+bx^2+cx+d$, so that the elementary symmetric polynomials $e_1=\alpha+\beta+\gamma=-b$, $e_2=\alpha\beta+\beta\gamma+\gamma\alpha=c$, $e_3=\alpha\beta\gamma=-d$ are, up to sign, exactly the coefficients.

fully symmetric — expressible

$\alpha^2+\beta^2+\gamma^2 = e_1^2-2e_2 = b^2-2c$.

Every one of the $6$ permutations of $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ leaves this expression looking identical.

only cyclic — not directly expressible

$\alpha^2\beta+\beta^2\gamma+\gamma^2\alpha$ survives the $3$ cyclic shifts, but swapping just $\alpha\leftrightarrow\beta$ turns it into a genuinely different quantity. It takes a resolvent — not a formula in $b,c,d$ — to pin down.

Fundamental Theorem of Symmetric Polynomials

Every polynomial in $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n$ that is unchanged by every permutation of the $\alpha_i$ can be written — uniquely — as a polynomial in $e_1,\dots,e_n$. Since each $e_k$ is, up to sign, a coefficient, every symmetric expression in the roots is automatically computable from the coefficients — without solving for a single root.

This is why Cardano's cubic formula works at all, and why it looks so strange when it does. Take $x^3-15x-4=0$: its discriminant is positive, guaranteeing three real roots, yet feeding $p=-15,q=-4$ into the formula demands $\sqrt{-121}$ — an imaginary detour. Carried through, it lands exactly on the real roots $4$ and $-2\pm\sqrt3$. Lagrange's insight was to build a deliberately almost-symmetric combination of the roots — a resolvent — whose cube is fully symmetric and hence computable, then unwind it. The same strategy resolves the quartic (Ferrari's resolvent cubic); applied to the quintic, the resolvent refuses to drop in degree at all — the first hint of Galois theory.

05 / The Central Idea

What Is an Algebraic Variety?

Now we can state the central idea. It is disarmingly simple:

An algebraic variety is the set of solutions to a system of polynomial equations.

That's it. A circle is an algebraic variety. A sphere is an algebraic variety. An elliptic curve is an algebraic variety. The set of points satisfying both

$$x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1, \qquad x + y + z = 0$$

(a great circle on the sphere — the intersection of a sphere and a plane) is also an algebraic variety.

The power of this definition is its generality. It works in any number of dimensions. It captures curves, surfaces, and objects with no easy geometric intuition — all under one roof.

06 / One Refinement

Not Every Zero Set Is One Shape

We met $V(xy)$ back at the start — the two coordinate axes, produced by a single equation. It splits cleanly into $V(x)\cup V(y)$, two smaller solution sets glued together. Call a solution set reducible when it splits like that, and irreducible when it cannot be split any further. Strictly, an algebraic variety is reserved for the irreducible case — one indivisible algebraic "atom." $V(x^2-y^2)=V(x-y)\cup V(x+y)$ is reducible for the same reason: it factors.

Irreducibility is not the same thing as smoothness. The curve below, $y^2=x^2(x+1)$, cannot be factored into lower-degree pieces — it is one irreducible variety — yet it crosses itself at the origin, a node.

$x^2-y^2=0$  (reducible)

$(x^2-1)y=0$  (3 components)

$y^2=x^2(x+1)$  (irreducible, singular)

07 / Patching a Gap

Affine Space Has a Blind Spot

Everything so far lives in ordinary — affine — space. But affine space has an awkward habit: parallel lines never meet, and a circle and a line can intersect in fewer points than a degree-counting theorem (Bézout's theorem: a conic and a line should meet in $2$ points) promises. The missing intersections have run off to infinity.

Projective space $\mathbb{P}^n$ patches this by formally adding points at infinity, using homogeneous coordinates $(a_0:\cdots:a_n)$ where scaling every coordinate by the same nonzero $\lambda$ gives back the same point. A projective variety is the zero locus of homogeneous polynomials inside $\mathbb{P}^n$.

Take the parabola $V(y-x^2)$. Introduce a third coordinate $z$, homogenize ($x\to x/z,\,y\to y/z$, then clear denominators), and it becomes $V(yz-x^2)\subset\mathbb{P}^2$. Setting $z=1$ recovers the original parabola; setting $z=0$ reveals what got added — the single point $(0:1:0)$, where the parabola's two "arms," which look like they run apart forever in the affine picture, meet tangentially at infinity.

ordinary space $K^n$; parallel lines never meet

Projective

Bridge

08 / Structure

The Dimension Question

A circle is 1-dimensional: you can move along it in one independent direction. A sphere is 2-dimensional: you can move in two independent directions on its surface. An algebraic variety has a well-defined dimension, and it relates to the number of variables and equations in a precise way:

$$\dim(V) \;=\; n - k \qquad \text{(under good conditions)}$$

where $n$ is the number of variables and $k$ is the number of independent equations.

n = 2, k = 1

09 / Where Things Break

Singularities

Most of the varieties we have seen are smooth — no sharp corners or cusps. But not all varieties behave so nicely.

Consider the curve defined by:

$$y^2 = x^3$$

At the origin, this curve comes to a sharp point — a cusp. The curve is not smooth there; there is no well-defined tangent line.

Singularities are where the geometry becomes complicated and interesting. Classifying and resolving them is a major theme in algebraic geometry — and the techniques developed for this purpose have turned out to be powerful far beyond their original context.

$y^2 = x^3$  (cusp)

10 / A Full Inventory

What Can a Variety Actually Look Like?

We have been meeting varieties one example at a time. It's worth pausing to ask the sharper question directly: in the smallest ambient spaces, $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{R}^2$, can we list every possible algebraic set?

On the line, $\mathbb{R}$

The answer here is completely finite and completely clean:

$S\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ is algebraic  $\iff$  $S$ is finite, or $S=\mathbb{R}$.

If $S=V(f_1,\dots,f_m)\neq\mathbb{R}$, some $f_i$ is not the zero polynomial — and a nonzero one-variable polynomial has at most $\deg f_i$ roots, so $S\subseteq V(f_i)$ is finite. Conversely $\mathbb{R}=V(0)$ and any finite set $\{a_1,\dots,a_k\}=V\big((x-a_1)\cdots(x-a_k)\big)$. The irreducible varieties are exactly the single points (each ideal $\langle x-a\rangle$ is maximal) and $\mathbb{R}$ itself — every larger finite set splits into point-varieties. This is the cleanest possible instance of the Zariski topology from further down this page: on a line, closed sets are precisely the finite sets and the whole line.

In the plane, $\mathbb{R}^2$

Here there is no short finite list — but there is real structure. Because $\mathbb{R}$ is an ordered field, a sum of squares vanishes only when every term does:

$$V(f_1,\dots,f_m) \;=\; V(f_1^2+\cdots+f_m^2).$$

So every real algebraic set is the zero locus of a single polynomial. (This trick fails completely over $\mathbb{C}$ — $i^2+1^2=0$ without $i=0$ — which is part of why algebraically closed fields behave so much more predictably, as the $x^2+y^2$ example already showed: a point over $\mathbb{R}$, two crossing lines over $\mathbb{C}$.) The question reduces to: what can $V(f)$ look like for one $f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]$?

Harnack's Curve Theorem caps how rich this can get: a smooth real plane curve of degree $d$ has at most $\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}{2}+1$ connected ovals, and the bound is sharp. Every algebraic subset of $\mathbb{R}^2$ has finitely many components, each an isolated point or a finite union of smooth arcs meeting at finitely many singular points — always tame in that sense. But because the oval count grows without bound as $d\to\infty$, there is no finite catalogue of shapes the way there was on the line; classifying real plane curves degree by degree is itself a research question, tied to Hilbert's 16th problem.

Is the open first quadrant a variety?

Take $Q=\{(x,y): x>0,\ y>0\}$ — no answer needs a single polynomial computation, just one topological fact. Every polynomial $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$ is continuous, so $V(f)=f^{-1}(\{0\})$ is closed, since $\{0\}$ is closed and preimages of closed sets under continuous maps are closed. An intersection of closed sets is closed, so every algebraic set is closed in $\mathbb{R}^n$ — no exceptions.

$Q$ is not closed: the sequence $(1/n,1/n)\in Q$ for every $n$, but its limit $(0,0)\notin Q$. So $Q$ cannot be an algebraic set.

$Q$ open, nonempty, proper  $\Longrightarrow$  $Q$ is not algebraic.

This argument is free of anything specific to quadrants: since $\mathbb{R}^n$ is connected, its only clopen subsets are $\varnothing$ and $\mathbb{R}^n$ itself, so no nonempty proper open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is ever algebraic. The closed quadrant $\{x\ge0,\,y\ge0\}$ isn't algebraic either — but for a different reason: it's cut out by polynomial inequalities, and no single equation can pin down a region with nonempty interior that is still a proper subset of the plane, since a variety containing an open piece of $\mathbb{R}^2$ must (by the dimension count of Chapter 05) be all of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

11 / Expanding Horizons

Beyond What You Can See

Here is a crucial point: algebraic varieties exist in any number of dimensions. A variety in 5-dimensional space defined by 2 polynomial equations has dimension 3. You cannot draw it, but it is just as mathematically real as a circle.

Moreover, algebraic geometers often work over fields other than the real numbers:

  • Over the complex numbers, a "curve" like $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ is actually a 2-dimensional surface (because $\mathbb{C}$ is 2-dimensional over $\mathbb{R}$). The geometry becomes richer.
  • Over finite fields, a variety has only finitely many points — yet their structure still encodes deep arithmetic information, connecting algebraic geometry to number theory.
  • Over the p-adic numbers, entirely new geometric landscapes appear, where "closeness" is measured not by distance but by divisibility.

This is where algebraic geometry stops being visual and starts being deeply algebraic — but the geometric intuition you have built from the low-dimensional examples continues to guide the way.

12 / Turning the Dictionary Around

Ideals: The Algebra Behind the Shape

Go in the opposite direction from $V$. Given a set of points $A$, collect every polynomial that vanishes on all of $A$:

$$I(A) = \{\, f : f(P) = 0 \text{ for all } P \in A \,\}$$

This set is an ideal: closed under addition, and absorbing under multiplication by anything in the polynomial ring. $V$ and $I$ now face each other across a mirror — a shape gives an ideal, an ideal gives back a shape.

$I(\{(1,2)\})$

Exactly the polynomials in $\langle x-1,\,y-2\rangle$ — every multiple of $x-1$ or $y-2$ kills the point, and nothing else does. A maximal ideal, matching the smallest possible variety.

$I(\text{parabola})$

Every polynomial vanishing on $V(y-x^2)$ is a multiple of $y-x^2$, so $I(V)=\langle y-x^2\rangle$ — a prime ideal, because the parabola is irreducible. Apply $V$ back to it, and you land on the parabola again.

Over an algebraically closed field, this correspondence is almost a perfect inverse — the content of Hilbert's Nullstellensatz:

Geometric objectAlgebraic object
irreducible varietyprime ideal
single pointmaximal ideal
union of two varietiesintersection of ideals

Push this one step further: the coordinate ring of the parabola is $K[x,y]/\langle y-x^2\rangle$. Because $y$ is forced to equal $x^2$ inside this quotient, every polynomial collapses to one in $x$ alone — $K[x,y]/\langle y-x^2\rangle \cong K[x]$, the coordinate ring of a plain line. Algebraically, the curved parabola and a straight line are indistinguishable; the curve is an artifact of how it sits in the plane, not of its intrinsic algebra.

13 / Connections

Why Does Any of This Matter?

Algebraic geometry might seem abstract, but it connects to surprisingly concrete things:

Cryptography

Elliptic curves over finite fields underpin modern cryptographic protocols — from TLS to blockchain signatures.

Physics

String theory uses Calabi-Yau manifolds — special algebraic varieties — to model the extra dimensions of spacetime.

Robotics

The set of configurations a robot arm can reach forms an algebraic variety. Understanding its shape tells you what the robot can and cannot do.

Coding Theory

Algebraic geometry codes are among the best error-correcting codes known, used in deep-space communication and data storage.

Machine Learning

Algebraic geometry helps analyse the structure of neural networks, parameter spaces, and optimisation landscapes.

Number Theory

Fermat's Last Theorem, the Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, and the Langlands program all rely on algebraic geometry.

14 / The Deeper View

Two Ideas That Changed Everything

The Zariski Topology

In ordinary topology (the kind used in analysis), open sets are like open intervals — they contain all points "nearby." The Zariski topology turns this on its head: the closed sets are precisely the algebraic varieties.

This means the open sets are the complements — "everything except where this polynomial vanishes." The Zariski topology is much coarser than the usual one. On a line, the Zariski closed sets are just finite sets of points and the whole line itself.

It seems too coarse to be useful — but it captures exactly the algebraic information, and that is what makes it powerful. Every continuous map in the Zariski topology turns out to be a polynomial map.

Schemes

If varieties are built from polynomial equations, schemes are built from rings. Every variety corresponds to a ring (its "coordinate ring"), and the scheme construction lets you reverse this: start with any commutative ring and get a geometric object.

This allows algebraic geometers to study number-theoretic objects — like the integers $\mathbb{Z}$ — as if they were geometric spaces. A prime number $p$ becomes a point in this space. This idea, called arithmetic geometry, is one of the most powerful in modern mathematics.

We will not go further into schemes here — that would require a full course. But the trajectory should be clear: from circles in the plane to geometric spaces built from abstract rings, the same fundamental idea — "polynomial equations define geometry" — keeps opening new doors.

Solve an equation, and you get points. Solve several, and you get a shape. Ask which shapes are indivisible, and you get varieties. Ask which polynomials a shape forces to vanish, and you get an ideal. The Nullstellensatz says these two questions have exactly the same answer — and everything from a school discriminant to a research-level moduli space is someone pushing that one correspondence further than the last person did.

Closing Thought

From Circles to Schemes

We started with a circle — the simplest non-trivial algebraic variety. We saw why coefficients can compute so much about roots without ever solving for them, moved to surfaces, then to curves of higher degree, then asked which shapes are truly indivisible and which live at infinity, then to singularities, ideals, higher dimensions and different number systems, and finally glimpsed the architecture of schemes.

At each step, the same basic idea — "solutions to polynomial equations" — opened up new territory. This is the flavour of algebraic geometry. It begins somewhere familiar and leads somewhere surprising.

If this glimpse has made you curious, that was the point.
