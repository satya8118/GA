1 · Complements and semidirect products

Let $G$ be a finite group of order $p^rm$, where $p$ is a prime and $p\nmid m$, and let $P$ be a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $G$. For a cyclic group of order $n=p^rm$ we have $\mathbb Z_n\cong\mathbb Z_{p^r}\times\mathbb Z_m$, so the group splits into a $p$-part and a $p'$-part. The natural question is whether every finite group does the same: is there a subgroup $N$ of order $m$ with $G=PN$?

Why ask for normalityA bare factorisation $G=PN$ is too weak to be interesting: orders alone can force it. For instance $S_4=D_4\cdot C_3$, since $|D_4||C_3|=24$ and $D_4\cap C_3=1$, with neither factor normal. Since Sylow subgroups are not normal in general, the only reasonable hope is that $N\trianglelefteq G$, so that $G\cong P\ltimes N$.
Definition · semidirect product

Let $H$ and $N$ be groups and let $\varphi:H\to\mathrm{Aut}(N)$ be a homomorphism. The (external) semidirect product $H\ltimes N$ is the set $H\times N$ with the group law

$$(h_1,n_1)(h_2,n_2)=\big(h_1h_2,\;n_1\,\varphi(h_1)(n_2)\big).$$

Internally: if $N\trianglelefteq G$ and $H\le G$ satisfy $H\cap N=1$ and $G=HN$, then $G\cong H\ltimes N$, where $H$ acts on $N$ by conjugation and the isomorphism is $(h,n)\mapsto nh$.

Intuition$H\ltimes N$ is “$N$ twisted by $H$”. When $\varphi$ is trivial there is no twist and we recover the direct product $H\times N$. Dihedral groups are $C_2\ltimes C_n$ with the generator of $C_2$ inverting $C_n$.
Definition · normal $p$-complementLet $G$ be a finite group of order $p^rm$, where $p$ is a prime and $p\nmid m$, and let $P$ be a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $G$. A subgroup $N$ of $G$ is called a normal $p$-complement of $P$ in $G$ if it satisfies the following three conditions:
  1. $N$ is a normal subgroup of $G$,
  2. $P\cap N=\{1\}$, and
  3. $G=PN$.
Counting elements, $|G|=|P||N|/|P\cap N|$ forces $|N|=m$, and $G$ is then the internal semidirect product $P\ltimes N$. We also say simply that “$G$ has a normal $p$-complement.”
Examples

$S_3$: $p=2$: $A_3$ is a normal 2-complement. $p=3$: would need a normal subgroup of order 2; none exists.

$S_4$: normal subgroups are $1,V_4,A_4,S_4$. A 2-complement has order 3 and a 3-complement has order 8, and neither order appears, so neither exists.

$A_4$: there are 8 elements of order 3, so with the identity the odd-order elements number 9, which does not divide 12: no normal 2-complement. But $V_4\trianglelefteq A_4$ is a normal 3-complement ($C_3$ acts on $V_4$ by an automorphism of order 3).

$D_n$: let $D_n$ be the dihedral group of order $2n$, with $n=2^rm$ and $m$ odd. The rotations of odd order form the cyclic subgroup $N=C_m$, which is a normal 2-complement (by Proposition 2.1 below, or directly since $|N|=m$ and $N\cap P=1$).

IntuitionIn each example the real question is whether $G$ “splits” into a $p$-part and a $p'$-part that do not interfere. In $A_4$ the normal $V_4$ is the $3'$-part and $C_3$ acts on it by rotating its three non-trivial elements, which is a genuine twist (not a direct product). In $S_4$ no normal piece of the right size exists, so the two parts cannot be separated.
Check yourself
Does $S_3$ factor as $PN$ with $p=3$?
Yes as sets ($C_3\cdot C_2=S_3$), but $C_2$ is not normal, so there is no normal 3-complement. Factorisation and normal complement are different things.

So normal complements sometimes exist and sometimes do not. To decide which, we now look for a criterion we can actually test.

2 · A criterion for existence

Our first goal is to turn the abstract question “does a normal $p$-complement exist?” into a concrete question about the elements of $G$. Throughout, $G$ is a finite group of order $p^rm$ with $p$ prime and $p\nmid m$, and $P$ is a Sylow $p$-subgroup. Call an element of $G$ a $p'$-element if its order is not divisible by $p$.

IntuitionIf $N$ is a normal $p$-complement, then $N$ is the “$p$-free part” of $G$: it has order $m$, so all its elements have order coprime to $p$, and it should swallow every element with that property. So the only candidate for a normal $p$-complement is the set of all $p'$-elements. The only question is whether that set is closed under multiplication. In general it is not: the $3$-cycles in $A_4$ all have order coprime to $2$, yet their products give the double transpositions, which have order $2$.
Proposition 2.1Let $G$ be a finite group of order $p^rm$ with $p\nmid m$, and let $P$ be a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $G$. Put $N:=\{x\in G : p\nmid \mathrm{ord}(x)\}$, the set of all $p'$-elements. Then $P$ has a normal $p$-complement if and only if $N$ is a subgroup of $G$. In that case $N$ is the normal $p$-complement.
Proof

($\Leftarrow$) Assume $N$ is a subgroup. We check the three conditions of a normal $p$-complement, and also that $|N|=m$.

$N$ is normal. For $g\in G$ and $x\in N$, the element $gxg^{-1}$ has the same order as $x$, so it lies in $N$.

$P\cap N=\{1\}$. Every element of $P$ has order a power of $p$. If such an element also lies in $N$, its order is coprime to $p$, so its order is $1$.

$p\nmid|N|$. If $p$ divided $|N|$, Cauchy’s theorem would give an element of order $p$ in $N$, which is impossible by the definition of $N$.

$G/N$ is a $p$-group. Suppose a prime $q\ne p$ divides $|G/N|$. By Cauchy’s theorem there is $yN\in G/N$ of order $q$. Then $y^q\in N$, so $y^q$ has some order $d$ with $p\nmid d$. Hence $\mathrm{ord}(y)$ divides $qd$, which is coprime to $p$, so $y\in N$. But then $yN=N$ is the identity, not an element of order $q$: a contradiction. So $|G/N|$ is a power of $p$.

Conclusion. Since $p\nmid|N|$ and $|G/N|$ is a power of $p$, we get $|N|=m$ and $|G/N|=p^r$. Then $|PN|=|P||N|/|P\cap N|=p^rm=|G|$, so $G=PN$. ∎

($\Rightarrow$) Assume $P$ has a normal $p$-complement $N'$, so $|N'|=m$ and $G/N'$ has order $p^r$. We show $N'$ equals the set $N$ of $p'$-elements, which shows that $N$ is a subgroup.

$N'\subseteq N$. By Lagrange the order of every element of $N'$ divides $m$, hence is coprime to $p$.

$N\subseteq N'$. Let $g\in G$ have order $k$ with $p\nmid k$. Then $gN'\in G/N'$ has order dividing $k$, and also dividing $|G/N'|=p^r$. These two numbers are coprime, so $gN'$ has order $1$, i.e. $g\in N'$. ∎

Check yourself
Use the proposition to show again that $A_4$ has no normal $2$-complement.
The $2'$-elements of $A_4$ are the identity and the eight $3$-cycles, nine elements in all. Since $9\nmid12$, they do not form a subgroup, so no normal $2$-complement exists.

Proposition 2.1 asks us to test a subset for being a subgroup. The next corollary rephrases the same fact as the existence of a map, which is far easier to produce.

IntuitionA normal subgroup is always the kernel of some quotient map. If $N$ is a normal $p$-complement, the quotient $G/N$ is a copy of $P$, so the map $G\to G/N\cong P$ is onto $P$ with kernel $N$. Conversely, any onto map $G\to P$ has a kernel of order $m$, which is the right size for a complement. So “find a complement” becomes “find a surjection onto $P$”.
Corollary 2.2Let $G$ be a finite group and $P$ a Sylow $p$-subgroup. Then $P$ has a normal $p$-complement if and only if there is a surjective group homomorphism $G\to P$.
Proof

($\Rightarrow$) Let $N$ be a normal $p$-complement. Since $G=PN$ and $P\cap N=1$, the second isomorphism theorem gives $G/N=PN/N\cong P/(P\cap N)=P$. Composing the quotient map $G\to G/N$ with this isomorphism gives a surjection $G\to P$.

($\Leftarrow$) Let $\varphi:G\to P$ be surjective with kernel $K$. Then $|K|=|G|/|P|=m$. We claim $K$ is exactly the set of $p'$-elements. Every element of $K$ has order dividing $m$, hence coprime to $p$. Conversely, let $g$ have order $k$ with $p\nmid k$. Then $\varphi(g)$ has order dividing $k$ and also dividing $|P|$, a power of $p$, so $\varphi(g)=1$ and $g\in K$. So the set of $p'$-elements is the subgroup $K$, and Proposition 2.1 gives a normal $p$-complement. ∎

3 · Burnside’s theorem

It remains to find a hypothesis under which the surjection $G\to P$ exists. Burnside’s hypothesis is stated with two standard subgroups attached to a subgroup $H\le G$.

Definition · centraliser

Let $G$ be a group and let $H$ be a subgroup of $G$. The centraliser of $H$ in $G$ is the set

$$C_G(H)=\{\,g\in G \;:\; gh=hg \text{ for every } h\in H\,\}.$$

In words, it consists of the elements of $G$ that commute with every element of $H$.

Example · centralisers in $S_3$

Take $G=S_3$. For a transposition such as $(12)$, conjugation acts by relabelling: $g(12)g^{-1}=(g(1)\;g(2))$. Checking the six elements of $S_3$, this equals $(12)$ only for $g=e$ and $g=(12)$. So

$$C_{S_3}\big(\langle(12)\rangle\big)=\{e,(12)\}.$$

For $H=A_3=\langle(123)\rangle$, the $3$-cycles commute with $(123)$, but a transposition conjugates $(123)$ to $(132)$, so it does not commute. Hence

$$C_{S_3}(A_3)=A_3.$$
Definition · normaliser

With $G$ and $H$ as above, the normaliser of $H$ in $G$ is the set

$$N_G(H)=\{\,g\in G \;:\; gHg^{-1}=H\,\}.$$

In words, it consists of the elements of $G$ that may move the individual elements of $H$ around, but send $H$ to itself as a whole.

Example · normalisers in $S_3$

For $H=\langle(12)\rangle=\{e,(12)\}$ we have $gHg^{-1}=\{e,\,g(12)g^{-1}\}$, which equals $H$ exactly when $g(12)g^{-1}=(12)$. So

$$N_{S_3}\big(\langle(12)\rangle\big)=\{e,(12)\}.$$

For $H=A_3$, which is a normal subgroup of $S_3$, every $g$ satisfies $gA_3g^{-1}=A_3$, so

$$N_{S_3}(A_3)=S_3.$$

Here the normaliser is strictly larger than the centraliser: the transpositions normalise $A_3$ (they send $(123)$ to $(132)$, which is still in $A_3$) but they do not centralise it.

How the two are related

Always $H\le N_G(H)$ and $C_G(H)\le N_G(H)$. Each $g\in N_G(H)$ gives an automorphism $h\mapsto ghg^{-1}$ of $H$, and this defines a homomorphism

$$N_G(H)\longrightarrow \mathrm{Aut}(H)$$

whose kernel is exactly $C_G(H)$. Therefore $N_G(H)/C_G(H)$ embeds in $\mathrm{Aut}(H)$, and in particular $[N_G(H):C_G(H)]$ divides $|\mathrm{Aut}(H)|$.

In $S_3$: for $H=A_3$ the quotient $S_3/A_3\cong C_2$ is all of $\mathrm{Aut}(C_3)$. For $H=\langle(12)\rangle$ the quotient is trivial, matching $\mathrm{Aut}(C_2)=1$.

Intuition

An element of $C_G(H)$ leaves every point of $H$ fixed under conjugation. An element of $N_G(H)$ is allowed to move points of $H$ around, as long as $H$ ends up as the same set. The quotient $N_G(H)/C_G(H)$ therefore records which symmetries of $H$ (that is, which automorphisms) are actually realised by conjugating from inside $G$. In the example above, the transpositions realise the inversion symmetry of $A_3\cong C_3$, and that is the only non-trivial symmetry $C_3$ has.

Theorem (Burnside, cited)Let $G$ be a finite group and $P$ a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $G$ with $C_G(P)=N_G(P)$. Then $P$ has a normal $p$-complement. The hypothesis is equivalent to $P\le Z(N_G(P))$.
IntuitionThe hypothesis says that no element of $G$ can normalise $P$ and act on it non-trivially: everything that normalises $P$ commutes with it, so $P$ sits inside $N_G(P)$ without any twisting. The transfer homomorphism (not developed here) turns this into the onto map $G\to P$ of Corollary 2.2. On $P$ it becomes $g\mapsto g^{[G:P]}$, an automorphism of the abelian group $P$ because $p\nmid[G:P]$. The key input is that two elements of $P$ which are conjugate in $G$ are already conjugate inside $N_G(P)$, and here that means they are equal.
Check yourself
Why does the hypothesis force $P$ to be abelian?
$P\le N_G(P)=C_G(P)$, so every element of $P$ commutes with every element of $P$.
Is the hypothesis necessary?No. Suppose a normal complement $N$ exists. Then $N_G(P)=P\,C_N(P)$ and $C_G(P)=Z(P)\,C_N(P)$, so $C_G(P)=N_G(P)$ holds if and only if $P$ is abelian. A non-abelian $p$-group (with $N=1$) therefore has a normal complement without satisfying the hypothesis. The exact criterion is Frobenius’s: $G$ has a normal $p$-complement iff $N_G(Q)/C_G(Q)$ is a $p$-group for every $p$-subgroup $Q$.
IntuitionBurnside’s theorem gives a clean sufficient condition that is easy to check with Sylow counting (as in Example 4.7 below). Frobenius’s criterion is the sharp statement but requires checking every $p$-subgroup. For applications one almost always wants the easy one.

4 · Applications

Burnside’s theorem is useful because $C_G(P)=N_G(P)$ can often be forced by counting alone. In this section we use it in five ways: to handle the smallest prime when its Sylow subgroup is cyclic (4.1), to show that groups with all Sylow subgroups cyclic are solvable (4.2–4.4), to constrain simple groups (4.5), to prove a strong converse of Lagrange’s theorem (4.6), and to rule out a specific order (4.7).

Proposition 4.1Let $G$ be a finite group and let $p$ be the smallest prime dividing $|G|$. If a Sylow $p$-subgroup $P$ of $G$ is cyclic, then $P$ has a normal $p$-complement.
IntuitionWrite $|P|=p^k$. The index $[N_G(P):C_G(P)]$ is the size of a group of automorphisms of $P$, so it divides $|\mathrm{Aut}(P)|=p^{k-1}(p-1)$. Its prime factors can only be $p$ or primes below $p$. Primes below $p$ do not divide $|G|$ because $p$ is the smallest prime, and the prime $p$ is excluded because $P$ is abelian. So there is no prime left to divide the index, and the index is $1$. Smallness of $p$ makes $\mathrm{Aut}(P)$ too small to be realised.
Proof

Let $|P|=p^k$. Since $P$ is cyclic, $\mathrm{Aut}(P)\cong(\mathbb Z/p^k)^\times$ has order $p^{k-1}(p-1)$, and $[N_G(P):C_G(P)]$ divides this. Suppose a prime $q$ divides the index. Then $q=p$ or $q\mid p-1$.

Case $q=p$. Since $P$ is abelian, $P\le C_G(P)$. Also $P$ is a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $N_G(P)$, so $|P|$ is the full $p$-part of $|N_G(P)|$. As $|P|$ divides $|C_G(P)|$, the index $[N_G(P):C_G(P)]$ is not divisible by $p$.

So no prime divides the index, i.e. $C_G(P)=N_G(P)$, and Burnside’s theorem gives a normal $p$-complement. ∎

Definition · solvable group

A group $G$ is called solvable if it has a chain of subgroups

$$1=G_0\trianglelefteq G_1\trianglelefteq\cdots\trianglelefteq G_n=G$$

in which each $G_{i-1}$ is normal in $G_i$ and every factor group $G_i/G_{i-1}$ is abelian. (Only $G_{i-1}\trianglelefteq G_i$ is required, not normality in all of $G$.)

Every abelian group is solvable (take $n=1$). For example $S_3$ is solvable via $1\trianglelefteq A_3\trianglelefteq S_3$ with factors $C_3$ and $C_2$, and $S_4$ is solvable via $1\trianglelefteq V_4\trianglelefteq A_4\trianglelefteq S_4$ with factors $V_4$, $C_3$, $C_2$. A non-abelian simple group such as $A_5$ is not solvable, since its only normal subgroups are $1$ and itself.

Intuition

A solvable group is one that can be built up from abelian groups by stacking layers, like an onion whose layers are all abelian. The next lemma says the stacking operation is safe: if the two pieces $N$ and $G/N$ are built from abelian layers, then so is $G$.

Lemma 4.2Let $G$ be a group and $N\trianglelefteq G$ a normal subgroup. If $N$ and $G/N$ are both solvable, then $G$ is solvable.
Proof (with a picture)

The idea is to build a series for $G$ by splicing two towers together: a tower for $N$ at the bottom, and a tower for $G/N$ pulled back to $G$ on top.

Step 1: the given towers. Since $N$ is solvable, there is a series $1=N_0\trianglelefteq N_1\trianglelefteq\cdots\trianglelefteq N_s=N$ with abelian factors. Since $G/N$ is solvable, there is a series $1=Q_0\trianglelefteq Q_1\trianglelefteq\cdots\trianglelefteq Q_t=G/N$ with abelian factors.

Step 2: pull the second tower back to $G$. Let $\pi:G\to G/N$ be the quotient map and put $G_j:=\pi^{-1}(Q_j)$ for $0\le j\le t$. Then $G_0=\pi^{-1}(1)=N$ and $G_t=\pi^{-1}(G/N)=G$.

Step 3: each new step is abelian. Fix $j\ge1$. The composite $G_j\xrightarrow{\ \pi\ }Q_j\longrightarrow Q_j/Q_{j-1}$ is surjective, and its kernel is exactly $\pi^{-1}(Q_{j-1})=G_{j-1}$. A kernel is always normal, so $G_{j-1}\trianglelefteq G_j$, and by the first isomorphism theorem $G_j/G_{j-1}\cong Q_j/Q_{j-1}$, which is abelian.

Step 4: stack. Since $G_0=N=N_s$, the two towers join end to end:

$$1=N_0\trianglelefteq\cdots\trianglelefteq N_s=N=G_0\trianglelefteq G_1\trianglelefteq\cdots\trianglelefteq G_t=G.$$

Every step has an abelian factor group, so $G$ is solvable. ∎

Proposition 4.3Let $G$ be a finite group all of whose Sylow subgroups are cyclic. Then $G$ is solvable.

The subgroup $N$ is smaller and again has only cyclic Sylow subgroups, so we can repeat. After finitely many peelings nothing is left, and each layer removed was cyclic.

Proof

We use induction on $|G|$. If $|G|=1$ the group is trivially solvable.

Suppose $|G|>1$ and let $p$ be the smallest prime dividing $|G|$, with Sylow $p$-subgroup $P$. By hypothesis $P$ is cyclic, so Proposition 4.1 gives a normal $p$-complement $N$. Then $G/N\cong P$ is cyclic, hence abelian, hence solvable.

Next, $N$ also has all Sylow subgroups cyclic: a Sylow $q$-subgroup of $N$ is a $q$-subgroup of $G$, so it lies inside a Sylow $q$-subgroup of $G$, and a subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic. Since $|N|<|G|$ (as $P\ne1$), the induction hypothesis shows $N$ is solvable.

Now $N\trianglelefteq G$ with $N$ and $G/N$ both solvable, so $G$ is solvable by Lemma 4.2. ∎

Corollary 4.4Every finite group of squarefree order is solvable.
Proof

If $|G|$ is squarefree, every prime $p$ dividing $|G|$ divides it exactly once. A Sylow $p$-subgroup then has order $p$, and every group of prime order is cyclic. So all Sylow subgroups of $G$ are cyclic, and $G$ is solvable by Proposition 4.3. ∎

Proposition 4.5If $G$ is non-abelian simple and $p$ is the smallest prime dividing $|G|$, then $p^3\mid|G|$ or $12\mid|G|$.
IntuitionA non-abelian simple group $G$ cannot have a normal $p$-complement for any prime $p$ dividing $|G|$, since that complement would be a proper non-trivial normal subgroup. So whenever Burnside’s hypothesis could hold, it must fail. If $|P|\le p^2$ then $P$ is abelian and automorphism counting leaves almost no room: for $|P|=p$ the index is $1$, and for $P\cong\mathbb Z_p^2$ the only way out is $p=2$ and a prime $3$ dividing the index. That is why a number as small as $12$ appears.

Proposition 4.6 · the converse of Lagrange’s theoremLet $G$ be a finite group all of whose Sylow subgroups are cyclic, and let $m$ divide $|G|$. Then (1) $G$ has a subgroup of order $m$, and (2) any two subgroups of order $m$ are conjugate in $G$.
Why this is remarkableLagrange says subgroup orders divide $|G|$, but the converse usually fails: $A_4$ has no subgroup of order $6$ (it would have index $2$, hence be normal, but $A_4$ has no index-2 subgroup). Here the Sylow subgroups of $A_4$ include the non-cyclic $V_4$, so the hypothesis fails, as it must. Cyclic Sylow subgroups make the group so well-behaved that every divisor is realised, and uniquely up to conjugacy.
StrategyInduct on $|G|$. Peel off the smallest prime: Proposition 4.1 gives a normal $p$-complement $N$ with $G/N\cong P$ cyclic. Split $m=p^km_0$. The $p'$-part $m_0$ is handled inside $N$ by induction. The $p$-part $p^k$ is attached using two tricks: (a) a $p$-group acting on a set whose size is not divisible by $p$ must have a fixed point, and (b) a cyclic group has exactly one subgroup of each order, so $p$-subgroups of the right order are forced to line up.
Step 0 · Setup and two facts

Induct on $|G|$ (the case $|G|=1$ is trivial). Let $p$ be the smallest prime dividing $|G|=p^rn_0$, $p\nmid n_0$, and $P$ a Sylow $p$-subgroup. By Proposition 4.1, $P$ has a normal complement $N$, with $|N|=n_0$ and $G/N\cong P$ cyclic.

Fact A. Every subgroup $L\le G$ also has all Sylow subgroups cyclic, because a Sylow subgroup of $L$ sits inside a Sylow subgroup of $G$ and subgroups of cyclic groups are cyclic. So the induction hypothesis applies to $N$ and to every proper subgroup of $G$.

Fact B. Write $m=p^km_0$ with $p\nmid m_0$. If $H\le G$ has order $m$, then $|H\cap N|=m_0$. Indeed $H/(H\cap N)\cong HN/N\le G/N$ is a $p$-group, so $[H:H\cap N]$ is a power of $p$, while $|H\cap N|$ divides $n_0$, which is coprime to $p$. In particular, if $p\nmid m$ then $H\le N$.

Step 1 · Existence of a subgroup of order $m$

Case $p\nmid m$. Then $m\mid n_0=|N|$, and induction in $N$ gives a subgroup of order $m$.

Case $p\mid m$. Write $m=p^km_0$ with $1\le k\le r$. Since $m_0\mid n_0$, induction gives subgroups of $N$ of order $m_0$. Let $X=\{H\le N:|H|=m_0\}\ne\emptyset$. By part (2) for $N$, the group $N$ acts transitively on $X$ by conjugation, so by orbit–stabiliser $|X|=[N:N_N(H)]$ divides $|N|$, hence $p\nmid|X|$.

Now $P$ also acts on $X$ by conjugation ($N$ is normal, so conjugating a subgroup of $N$ of order $m_0$ gives another one). Every orbit of a $p$-group has size a power of $p$. The orbit sizes add up to $|X|$, which is not divisible by $p$, so some orbit has size $1$. That is a subgroup $H_0\in X$ with $P\le N_G(H_0)$.

$P$ is a $p$-group of order $p^r$ and $k\le r$, so $P$ has a subgroup $P_k$ of order $p^k$. Since all of $P$ normalises $H_0$, so does $P_k$. Hence $P_kH_0$ is a subgroup. Since $\gcd(p^k,m_0)=1$ we get $P_k\cap H_0=1$, so $|P_kH_0|=p^km_0=m$. ∎

Where did we use that $p$ is the smallest prime?
Only to get the normal $p$-complement $N$ from Proposition 4.1 (via Burnside). Everything after that uses cyclicity of Sylow subgroups, the $p$-group fixed-point count, and induction.
Step 2 · Conjugacy of subgroups of order $m$

Let $H,K\le G$ both have order $m$.

Case $p\nmid m$. By Fact B, $H,K\le N$, so they are conjugate in $N$ by induction.

Case $p\mid m$. By Fact B, $|H\cap N|=|K\cap N|=m_0$. By induction in $N$, these are conjugate by some $x\in N$. Replace $K$ by $xKx^{-1}$ (still of order $m$) so that $H\cap N=K\cap N=:M$.

Since $N\trianglelefteq G$, we have $M=H\cap N\trianglelefteq H$ and $M=K\cap N\trianglelefteq K$. So both $H$ and $K$ normalise $M$, hence so does $L:=\langle H,K\rangle$.

If $L\ne G$: $L$ is a proper subgroup containing $H,K$, with cyclic Sylow subgroups (Fact A) and $m\mid|L|$. By induction, $H$ and $K$ are conjugate in $L$, hence in $G$. Undoing the replacement of $K$, the original $K$ is conjugate to $H$ in $G$ as well.

If $L=G$: then $M\trianglelefteq G$. Work in $\bar G=G/M$. The group $\bar H=H/M$ has order $p^k$, and so does $\bar K$. A Sylow $p$-subgroup of $\bar G$ has order $p^r$ (as $M$ is a $p'$-group) and is the image of $P$, since $PM/M\cong P/(P\cap M)\cong P$; so it is cyclic. Now $\bar H$ lies in some Sylow $p$-subgroup $S$ and $\bar K$ in some $S'=gSg^{-1}$. Then $g\bar Hg^{-1}$ and $\bar K$ are both subgroups of order $p^k$ of the cyclic group $S'$, which has only one such subgroup. So $g\bar Hg^{-1}=\bar K$. Taking preimages (both $H$ and $K$ contain $M$) gives $\tilde gH\tilde g^{-1}=K$ for any $\tilde g\in G$ mapping to $g\in\bar G$. ∎

Where exactly is cyclicity of the Sylow subgroups used?
Three places. (i) In Proposition 4.1, to obtain the normal complement $N$. (ii) In Fact A, so that induction applies to $N$ and to proper subgroups. (iii) In Step 2, where a cyclic Sylow subgroup has a unique subgroup of order $p^k$, which forces $\bar H$ and $\bar K$ to match. Step 1 itself needs only that $P$ is a $p$-group. For $A_4$, the non-cyclic $V_4$ has three subgroups of order $2$, uniqueness fails, and indeed $A_4$ has no subgroup of order $6$.
Example 4.7 · order 552 $=2^3\cdot3\cdot23$Let $P$ be a Sylow 23-subgroup. $n_{23}\mid24$ and $n_{23}\equiv1\pmod{23}$, so $n_{23}\in\{1,24\}$. If it is 1, $P$ is normal. If 24, then $[G:N_G(P)]=24=[G:P]$, so $N_G(P)=P=C_G(P)$ (as $P$ is abelian). Burnside gives a normal 23-complement of order 24. Either way $G$ is not simple.
IntuitionSylow counting shows that either $P$ is normal, or $N_G(P)=P$. In the second case $N_G(P)$ is as small as it can be, so there is no room for any element outside $P$ to normalise $P$ and twist it. Since $P$ is abelian, $C_G(P)=N_G(P)$ and Burnside applies. The same pattern works whenever a Sylow subgroup of prime order is forced by counting to be self-normalising.
History

After proving Proposition 4.5 in 1895, Burnside made two conjectures about the orders of non-abelian simple groups.

The first was that every non-abelian simple group has even order, that is, $p=2$ in Proposition 4.5. This was proved in the early 1960s by Walter Feit and John Thompson, and the published proof is 255 pages long.

The second was that $12$ divides the order of every non-abelian simple group. This was shown to be false in 1962 by Michio Suzuki, who found simple groups whose order is not divisible by $3$.
