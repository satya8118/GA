Seven Lectures on Field Extensions

Lecture 1
Extensions, Degree & the Tower Law

Big fields, small fields — and how to measure the gap.

§1 What is a field extension?

Definition A field $\mathbb{K}$ is an extension of a field $\mathbb{F}$ if $\mathbb{K}$ contains $\mathbb{F}$, i.e. $\mathbb{F}$ is a subfield of $\mathbb{K}$. We write $\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F}$.

Familiar examples: $\mathbb{Q}\subset\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{C}$. Also $\mathbb{Q}\subset\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt2]=\{a+b\sqrt2: a,b\in\mathbb{Q}\}$.

Question 1 Is $\mathbb{Z}_3$ an extension of $\mathbb{Z}_2$? Is $\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt2]$ an extension of $\mathbb{Q}$?
$\mathbb{Z}_3$ is not an extension of $\mathbb{Z}_2$: $\mathbb{Z}_2$ is not even a subset of $\mathbb{Z}_3$ as a subfield ($1+1=0$ in $\mathbb{Z}_2$ but $1+1=2\neq0$ in $\mathbb{Z}_3$). Fields of different characteristic can never be nested. Yes, $\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt2]/\mathbb{Q}$ is an extension.

§2 Extensions are vector spaces

Fact If $\mathbb{K}$ is an extension of $\mathbb{F}$, then $\mathbb{K}$ is a vector space over $\mathbb{F}$: add elements of $\mathbb{K}$, multiply by scalars from $\mathbb{F}\subseteq\mathbb{K}$.
Definition — degree The dimension of $\mathbb{K}$ over $\mathbb{F}$ is the degree $[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}]$. If it is finite, $\mathbb{K}$ is a finite extension; otherwise an infinite extension.

Question 2 Why is $[\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{Q}]=\infty$?
Count: how many elements does a finite-dimensional $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space have?
If $[\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{Q}]=n$ then $\mathbb{R}\cong\mathbb{Q}^n$, which is countable. But $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable. Hence $[\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{Q}]=\infty$. (Another route: $1,\pi,\pi^2,\dots$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb{Q}$.)

Question 4 Give an example of a non-zero vector space with only finitely many elements.
$\mathbb{Z}_p$ ($p$ prime) is a vector space over itself with exactly $p$ elements. More generally $\mathbb{Z}_p^n$ has $p^n$ elements. A finite field always yields such an example.

§3 The Tower Law

Theorem 1 Let $\mathbb{F}\subseteq\mathbb{K}\subseteq\mathbb{L}$. If $\mathbb{L}$ is finite over $\mathbb{K}$ and $\mathbb{K}$ is finite over $\mathbb{F}$, then $\mathbb{L}$ is finite over $\mathbb{F}$ and $$[\mathbb{L}:\mathbb{F}]=[\mathbb{L}:\mathbb{K}]\,[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}].$$
Question 5 Prove the Tower Law.
Take a basis $x_1,\dots,x_m$ of $\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F}$ and a basis $y_1,\dots,y_n$ of $\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{K}$. Show that the $mn$ products $x_iy_j$ form a basis of $\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{F}$.
Spanning. Let $l\in\mathbb{L}$. Then $l=\sum_j k_j y_j$ with $k_j\in\mathbb{K}$, and each $k_j=\sum_i f_{ij}x_i$ with $f_{ij}\in\mathbb{F}$. So $l=\sum_{i,j}f_{ij}\,x_iy_j$.
Independence. If $\sum_{i,j}f_{ij}x_iy_j=0$, group as $\sum_j\big(\sum_i f_{ij}x_i\big)y_j=0$. The $y_j$ are independent over $\mathbb{K}$, so each $\sum_i f_{ij}x_i=0$; the $x_i$ are independent over $\mathbb{F}$, so every $f_{ij}=0$.
Hence $\{x_iy_j\}$ is a basis with $mn$ elements. ∎
Question 6 If $[\mathbb{L}:\mathbb{F}]=7$ (prime), how many fields can lie strictly between $\mathbb{F}$ and $\mathbb{L}$?
None. If $\mathbb{F}\subseteq\mathbb{K}\subseteq\mathbb{L}$ then $[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}]$ divides $7$, so it is $1$ ($\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{F}$) or $7$ ($\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{L}$). This is exactly the next theorem.
Theorem 2 If $\mathbb{L}$ is a finite extension of $\mathbb{F}$ and $\mathbb{K}$ is a subfield of $\mathbb{L}$ containing $\mathbb{F}$, then $[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}]$ divides $[\mathbb{L}:\mathbb{F}]$.
Question 7 Prove Theorem 2.
$\mathbb{K}\subseteq\mathbb{L}$ is an $\mathbb{F}$-subspace of a finite-dimensional space, so $[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}]<\infty$. An $\mathbb{F}$-basis of $\mathbb{L}$ spans $\mathbb{L}$ over $\mathbb{K}$ as well, so $[\mathbb{L}:\mathbb{K}]<\infty$. By the Tower Law $[\mathbb{L}:\mathbb{F}]=[\mathbb{L}:\mathbb{K}][\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}]$, so $[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}]$ divides $[\mathbb{L}:\mathbb{F}]$. ∎
Question 8 Can there be a field $\mathbb{K}$ with $\mathbb{Q}\subset\mathbb{K}\subset\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt[3]{2}]$ and $\mathbb{K}$ different from both? (Assume $[\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt[3]{2}]:\mathbb{Q}]=3$.)
No. $[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{Q}]$ must divide $3$, so it is $1$ or $3$, forcing $\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}$ or $\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt[3]2]$.

§4 Building fields from polynomials

Theorem 3 Let $f(x)\in\mathbb{F}[x]$ be irreducible of degree $n$. Then $\mathbb{F}[x]/\langle f(x)\rangle$ is a field, it contains (a copy of) $\mathbb{F}$, and it is a finite extension of $\mathbb{F}$ of degree $n$.
Question 9 Why is $\mathbb{F}[x]/\langle f\rangle$ a field when $f$ is irreducible?
$\mathbb{F}[x]$ is a PID. In a PID, what do irreducible elements generate?
In the PID $\mathbb{F}[x]$, $f$ irreducible $\Rightarrow$ $\langle f\rangle$ is a maximal ideal. Indeed if $\langle f\rangle\subseteq\langle g\rangle$ then $g\mid f$, so $g$ is a unit or an associate of $f$. A quotient by a maximal ideal is a field. The constants $c+\langle f\rangle$ give a copy of $\mathbb{F}$.

Question 10 Identify $\mathbb{R}[x]/\langle x^2+1\rangle$. What is its degree over $\mathbb{R}$?
$x^2+1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$, so the quotient is a field with basis $\{1,\bar x\}$ and $\bar x^2=-1$. It is isomorphic to $\mathbb{C}$, degree $2$ — the complex numbers are born from a polynomial!
Question 11 Show that $\mathbb{Z}_2[x]/\langle x^2+x+1\rangle$ is a field with exactly 4 elements. List them.
$x^2+x+1$ has no root in $\mathbb{Z}_2$ ($f(0)=f(1)=1$) and has degree 2, so it is irreducible. The degree is 2, so there are $2^2=4$ elements: $0,\ 1,\ \bar x,\ 1+\bar x$, with $\bar x^2=\bar x+1$.

§5 Algebraic and transcendental elements

Definition (Lecture 2) Let $\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F}$. An element $a\in\mathbb{K}$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{F}$ if there are $\alpha_0,\dots,\alpha_n\in\mathbb{F}$, not all zero, with $\alpha_0+\alpha_1a+\dots+\alpha_na^n=0$.
Question 12 Why is every $a\in\mathbb{F}$ algebraic over $\mathbb{F}$?
$a$ is a root of the non-zero polynomial $x-a\in\mathbb{F}[x]$.
Definition — transcendental An element $a\in\mathbb{K}$ that is not algebraic over $\mathbb{F}$ is called transcendental over $\mathbb{F}$: no non-zero polynomial in $\mathbb{F}[x]$ vanishes at $a$, i.e. $1,a,a^2,\dots$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb{F}$. Examples over $\mathbb{Q}$: $\pi$ and $e$ (hard theorems of Lindemann and Hermite), while $\sqrt2,\ i,\ \sqrt[3]{5}$ are algebraic.
Question 13 Do the algebraic elements of $\mathbb{K}$ over $\mathbb{F}$ form a field? In particular, if $a,b$ are algebraic, are $a\pm b$, $ab$, $a/b$ algebraic?
Consider $\mathbb{F}[a,b]$. How large is it as an $\mathbb{F}$-vector space?
Yes. If $a$ has degree $m$ and $b$ degree $n$, then $\mathbb{F}[a,b]$ is spanned over $\mathbb{F}$ by $a^ib^j$ ($i<m,\ j<n$), so it is a finite extension of degree $\le mn$. Every element of a finite extension is algebraic (shown below), and $a\pm b,\ ab,\ a/b$ all lie in $\mathbb{F}[a,b]$. So the algebraic elements form a subfield (full details in Lecture 2).
Question 14 The set of algebraic numbers (elements of $\mathbb{C}$ algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$) is countable. Is every element of $\mathbb{C}$ algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$? Is every element of $\mathbb{C}$ algebraic over $\mathbb{R}$?
Over $\mathbb{R}$: for $z=x+iy$ find a real quadratic with root $z$.
Over $\mathbb{Q}$: no. $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ is countable and each non-zero polynomial has finitely many roots, so the algebraic numbers are countable. But $\mathbb{C}$ is uncountable, so transcendental numbers exist — in fact "almost all" complex numbers are transcendental.
Over $\mathbb{R}$: yes. For $z=x+iy$, the real polynomial $t^2-2x\,t+(x^2+y^2)$ has $z$ as a root. (Also $[\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}]=2$ is finite, so $\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}$ is algebraic.) Same field $\mathbb{C}$, different base fields, different answers!
Definition — algebraic extension An extension $\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F}$ is an algebraic extension if every element of $\mathbb{K}$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{F}$. Otherwise it contains a transcendental element.
Question 15 Is every finite extension an algebraic extension?
If $[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}]=n$, how many elements can a linearly independent set in $\mathbb{K}$ have?
Yes. Let $[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}]=n$ and $a\in\mathbb{K}$. The $n+1$ elements $1,a,\dots,a^n$ are linearly dependent over $\mathbb{F}$, so $\alpha_0+\alpha_1a+\dots+\alpha_na^n=0$ with $\alpha_i\in\mathbb{F}$ not all zero. Thus $a$ is algebraic. ∎
Question 16 What about the converse: is every algebraic extension finite?
Let $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ be all algebraic numbers. Does it contain $\sqrt[n]{2}$ for every $n$?
No. $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q}$ is algebraic by definition, yet infinite: for each $n$ it contains $\sqrt[n]{2}$, whose minimal polynomial $x^n-2$ (Eisenstein) has degree $n$, so $[\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt[n]2]:\mathbb{Q}]=n$. If $[\overline{\mathbb{Q}}:\mathbb{Q}]$ were finite, Theorem 2 would bound all these $n$. Contradiction.
Hence: finite ⇒ algebraic, but algebraic ⇏ finite.

Question 17 Classify each as (finite) / (infinite algebraic) / (not algebraic): $\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}$, $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{Q}(\pi)/\mathbb{Q}$.
$\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}$: finite (degree 2). $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q}$: infinite but algebraic. $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{Q}(\pi)/\mathbb{Q}$: not algebraic (contain the transcendental $\pi$), hence infinite.

§6 Problems with solutions

Problem A Give an irreducible polynomial over any field $\mathbb{F}$.
$x$ (or $x-a$ for any $a\in\mathbb{F}$). A degree-1 polynomial cannot be a product of two polynomials of lower degree. Over $\mathbb{Z}_2$, $x$ and $x+1$ are the only irreducibles of degree 1.
Problem B Is there an irreducible polynomial of degree 3 in $\mathbb{R}[x]$?
Think of the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra and Theorem 2 with $[\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}]=2$.
No. Three proofs:
Proof 1 (degrees). A real cubic has a root $\alpha\in\mathbb{C}$ (Fundamental Theorem of Algebra). If $f$ were irreducible, $[\mathbb{R}[\alpha]:\mathbb{R}]=3$ would have to divide $[\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}]=2$ — impossible.
Proof 2 (conjugate roots). For $f\in\mathbb{R}[x]$, $f(z)=0\Rightarrow f(\bar z)=\overline{f(z)}=0$, so non-real roots come in conjugate pairs. A cubic has 3 roots (with multiplicity), so at least one is real: $f(r)=0$, hence $(x-r)\mid f$ and $f$ is reducible.
Proof 3 (Intermediate Value Theorem). If $f$ is monic of degree 3, $f(x)\to-\infty$ as $x\to-\infty$ and $\to+\infty$ as $x\to+\infty$, so $f$ has a real root $r$, and $(x-r)$ divides $f$.
Consequently the only irreducible polynomials over $\mathbb{R}$ have degree 1 or 2 (degree 2 with negative discriminant). The same argument works for every odd degree $>1$.
Problem C Give a polynomial of degree $n$ in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.
$x^n-2$, by Eisenstein's criterion with $p=2$ ($2\nmid1$, $2\mid0,\dots,2\mid 2$ at the constant term, $4\nmid2$).
Problem D State Eisenstein's criterion.
Let $f=a_0+a_1x+\dots+a_nx^n\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$. If for some prime $p$: $p\nmid a_n$; $p\mid a_0,a_1,\dots,a_{n-1}$; and $p^2\nmid a_0$, then $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.

§8 SAQ — Short Answer Questions

Answer in a line or two on paper first, then reveal.

SAQ 1Define the degree of an extension $\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{F}$.
The dimension of $\mathbb{K}$ as a vector space over $\mathbb{F}$, written $[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}]$.
SAQ 2State the Tower Law.
For $\mathbb{F}\subseteq\mathbb{K}\subseteq\mathbb{L}$ with finite degrees, $[\mathbb{L}:\mathbb{F}]=[\mathbb{L}:\mathbb{K}][\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}]$.
SAQ 3Can $[\mathbb{L}:\mathbb{F}]=9$ and a subfield $\mathbb{K}\supseteq\mathbb{F}$ have $[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{F}]=2$?
No, since $2\nmid9$.
SAQ 4What is $[\mathbb{F}[x]/\langle f\rangle:\mathbb{F}]$ for irreducible $f$ of degree $n$?
$n$, with basis $1,\bar x,\dots,\bar x^{n-1}$.
SAQ 5Is $\mathbb{Q}$ a subfield of $\mathbb{Z}_7$? Why?
No. $\mathbb{Z}_7$ has characteristic 7, $\mathbb{Q}$ has characteristic 0.
SAQ 6Why is $\mathbb{Z}_2[x]/\langle x^2+1\rangle$ not a field?
$x^2+1=(x+1)^2$ over $\mathbb{Z}_2$ is reducible, so $\langle x^2+1\rangle$ is not maximal; $(x+1)$ is a zero divisor in the quotient.
SAQ 8Define a transcendental element and give an example over $\mathbb{Q}$.
An element not algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$: no non-zero rational polynomial vanishes at it. Example: $\pi$ or $e$.
SAQ 9Is $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q}$ finite? Is it algebraic?
Algebraic, yes; finite, no (it contains $\sqrt[n]2$ of degree $n$ for every $n$).
SAQ 7Give an infinite extension of $\mathbb{Q}$.
$\mathbb{R}$ (or $\mathbb{Q}(x)$, the field of rational functions).
