Geometry and Arithmetic

From a rational point on a conic to an addition law on a cubic.
§1.1 Conics§1.2 Cubics§1.3 Weierstrass form§1.4 AdditionQuick quiz
Chapter roadmapThere are two geometric ideas to keep separate. A line through one known point on a conic determines a second point; this yields a rational parametrization. A line through two known points on a cubic determines a third; after choosing an origin, this yields the elliptic-curve group operation. The shift from degree two to degree three changes the arithmetic profoundly.

§1.1 Rational Points on Conics

DefinitionsA point $P=(x,y)$ is rational if $x,y\in\mathbb Q$. A rational line has an equation $ux+vy+w=0$ with $u,v,w\in\mathbb Q$; a rational conic is the zero set of a degree-two polynomial with rational coefficients, for example $$Ax^2+Bxy+Cy^2+Dx+Ey+F=0.$$ Intersecting two rational lines gives a rational point (when unique). A rational line and a rational conic, however, usually give a quadratic equation, so their intersections need not both be rational.
Rational second-intersection principleIf a rational line meets a rational conic in two points and one intersection is known to be rational, the other is rational as well, counting multiplicity. Reason: after substitution, the intersection parameter is a root of a quadratic polynomial over $\mathbb Q$; the sum of the two roots is rational.
The conic projection ideaChoose one known rational point $O$ on a nonsingular conic. For each rational slope, draw a line through $O$. Its other intersection with the conic is rational. Conversely, a rational point $P\neq O$ determines a rational slope. On a projective line this gives a parametrization (with the usual caveat that a chosen affine coordinate may omit a point).
Worked derivation: the unit circleStart at $(-1,0)$ on $x^2+y^2=1$. Give a line through it slope $t$: $$y=t(x+1).$$ Substitute: $1-x^2=t^2(x+1)^2$. Cancelling the known factor $x+1$ at the other intersection gives $$\boxed{x=\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2},\qquad y=\frac{2t}{1+t^2}}.$$ A rational $t$ produces rational coordinates. For $x\ne-1$, the inverse parameter is $t=y/(1+x)$. The omitted point $(-1,0)$ corresponds to the projective parameter $t=\infty$.

Interactive lab · Pythagorean triplesWrite $t=m/n$. Then $x=(n^2-m^2)/(n^2+m^2)$ and $y=2mn/(n^2+m^2)$. Choose integers $n>m>0$; to get a primitive right triangle, take $m,n$ coprime and of opposite parity.
Question 1.1Why is it not enough to take the point where any rational line crosses a rational conic to guarantee a rational intersection?
Think about the quadratic $x^2-2=0$.
Rational coefficients do not force the roots of a quadratic to be rational. The second-intersection argument requires one already known rational root; then the other root is the rational sum of roots minus the known one.
Question 1.2Choose $t=2/3$. Find the corresponding point on the unit circle and verify the equation.
$x=(1-4/9)/(1+4/9)=5/13$, $y=(4/3)/(13/9)=12/13$; hence $x^2+y^2=(25+144)/169=1$.
A subtle contrast: existence is a separate problemParametrization works after a rational point is known. A general rational conic may have none. The book introduces Legendre's criterion for diagonal ternary quadratic equations, and the real and $p$-adic local-solvability viewpoint of the Hasse principle. Do not confuse finding all points given one with deciding if any point exists.

§1.2 The Geometry of Cubic Curves

Why cubics behave differentlyA line ordinarily meets a nonsingular plane cubic in three points over an algebraic closure, counting intersection multiplicity. A rational line through two rational points $P,Q$ has a third intersection $R$ that is rational: after substituting the line, a rational cubic polynomial has two known rational roots, so the remaining root is rational. Tangency plays the role of joining a point to itself.
From the third-intersection operation to additionWrite $P*Q$ for the third intersection of the cubic with the line through $P,Q$. This alone is not a group law. Choose a smooth rational base point $O$ and define $$\boxed{P+Q=O*(P*Q).}$$ On a nonsingular projective cubic, this operation is commutative, has identity $O$ and inverses; its associativity takes real work. The book motivates the associativity using the classical nine-point intersection geometry and postpones the rigorous underlying geometry to Appendix A.
The nine-point principle (geometric motivation)Two cubics meet in nine points counting multiplicity in the projective plane, under suitable hypotheses. Any cubic passing through eight of their intersection points also passes through the ninth. This is the geometric device behind the associative law; in §1.2 the authors explicitly offer an intuitive rather than fully foundational argument.
Why one rational point is not enough to parametrize a cubicProjection from one point on a cubic generally leaves a quadratic equation for the other intersections, unlike the conic case. Some cubic curves have points locally everywhere but no rational point globally. The book's explicit example is the projective cubic $3X^3+4Y^3+5Z^3=0$, demonstrating failure of the naive local-to-global principle.
Mordell's theorem (announced here)For a nonsingular cubic defined over $\mathbb Q$ with a rational base point, its rational points form a finitely generated abelian group. Thus there exist finitely many points $P_1,\dots,P_r$ such that every rational point is an integral linear combination of these points and finitely many torsion elements. Chapter 3 addresses the proof strategy.
Question 1.3Why must the third intersection of a rational cubic and rational line be rational if the first two intersections are rational?
The substitution gives a polynomial with rational coefficients of degree three. Dividing out two rational linear factors leaves a rational linear factor; its zero is rational. This includes multiplicities, so a tangent at a rational point also supplies the needed double intersection.
Question 1.4Does the statement “a cubic has a rational point” alone imply that every rational point can be written as a rational function of one parameter?
No. Smooth plane cubics have genus one and are not rationally parametrized by a single affine-line parameter. The chord-and-tangent process defines a group instead of a conic-style parametrization. Singular cubics are a distinct case.

§1.3 Weierstrass Normal Form

The model used throughout Chapters 1–4After suitable coordinate changes over a field of characteristic different from $2$ and $3$ (under the appropriate smoothness and rational-point assumptions), a smooth plane cubic with rational origin can be written in Weierstrass form. The book primarily uses $$\boxed{E:\ y^2=f(x)=x^3+ax^2+bx+c,\quad a,b,c\in\mathbb Q.}$$ The distinguished identity is the projective point at infinity $O=[0:1:0]$.
Projective completion and the one point at infinityReplace $x=X/Z$, $y=Y/Z$. The homogeneous equation is $$Y^2Z=X^3+aX^2Z+bXZ^2+cZ^3.$$ Setting $Z=0$ forces $X=0$; only the projective point $[0:1:0]$ remains. A vertical line meets the curve at $(x,y)$, $(x,-y)$ and $O$. Therefore $-(x,y)=(x,-y)$, and $P+(-P)=O$.
Smoothness conditionLet $F(x,y)=y^2-f(x)$. A singular affine point must satisfy $F=\partial F/\partial x=\partial F/\partial y=0$. Thus $y=0$ and $f(x)=f'(x)=0$. A Weierstrass cubic over characteristic $\ne2$ is nonsingular exactly when $f$ has no repeated root, equivalently when its cubic discriminant is nonzero.
Real pictures: one branch or two componentsIf the cubic polynomial $f$ has one real root, the real elliptic curve has one connected component (the point at infinity closes it). If $f$ has three distinct real roots, its real locus has two components. Smoothness excludes multiple roots. The real shape is not the same as the group-theoretic structure, but it guides the geometric addition picture.
Singular does not mean an elliptic curveExamples such as $y^2=x^3$ (a cusp) and $y^2=x^3+x^2$ (a node) have repeated roots of $f$ and fail the smoothness condition. They have rational parametrizations and different arithmetic. Chapter 3.7 revisits their smooth-point groups.
Question 1.5Show $y^2=x^3-x$ is nonsingular but $y^2=x^3$ is singular.
Look for common zeros of $f$ and $f'$.
For $f=x^3-x$, the three roots $-1,0,1$ are distinct, so $f$ and $f'=3x^2-1$ have no common root. For $f=x^3$, both $f$ and $f'=3x^2$ vanish at $x=0$; $(0,0)$ is a singular point.

§1.4 Explicit Formulas for the Group Law

Addition formulasLet $E:y^2=x^3+ax^2+bx+c$ and $P=(x_1,y_1)$, $Q=(x_2,y_2)$.
  • Identity: $P+O=P$.
  • Inverses: if $Q=-P=(x_1,-y_1)$, then $P+Q=O$.
  • Distinct points with $x_1\neq x_2$: slope $\lambda=(y_2-y_1)/(x_2-x_1)$.
  • Doubling when $y_1\neq0$: tangent slope $\lambda=(3x_1^2+2ax_1+b)/(2y_1)$.
  • In either finite-slope case: $$\boxed{\begin{aligned}x_3&=\lambda^2-a-x_1-x_2,\\ y_3&=\lambda(x_1-x_3)-y_1.\end{aligned}}$$ The sum is $(x_3,y_3)$; the third intersection has $y$-coordinate $-y_3$.
  • If $P=Q$ and $y_1=0$, the tangent is vertical and $2P=O$.
Why the formula worksA nonvertical line through $P,Q$ is $y=\lambda x+\nu$. Substitution into $y^2=f(x)$ gives $$x^3+(a-\lambda^2)x^2+(b-2\lambda\nu)x+(c-\nu^2)=0.$$ Its three roots are $x_1,x_2,x_R$. Vieta's formula yields $x_R=\lambda^2-a-x_1-x_2$. The group sum reflects $R=(x_R,\lambda x_R+\nu)$ across the $x$-axis, giving the displayed $y_3$.
Figure 1.4 lab: chord and reflection
For $E:y^2=x^3-x+1$, $P=(0,1)$ and $Q=(1,1)$ lie on the line $y=1$; the third intersection is $R=(-1,1)$, so $P+Q=(-1,-1)$. The dashed segment illustrates reflection across the $x$-axis.
Book example: rational additionOn $E:y^2=x^3+17$, take $P=(-1,4)$ and $Q=(2,5)$. Then $\lambda=\frac13$, $\nu=\frac{13}3$; hence $$\boxed{P+Q=\left(-\frac89,-\frac{109}{27}\right).}$$ This illustrates that rational inputs give rational outputs, often with growing denominators.
Duplication formulaEliminating the tangent slope from the doubling formula yields $$\boxed{x(2P)=\frac{x^4-2bx^2-8cx+b^2-4ac}{4(x^3+ax^2+bx+c)}}.$$ The denominator is $4y^2$; it vanishes precisely when $P$ is a nonidentity point of order two. This formula will appear again in torsion computations and in the height estimates.
Interactive lab · exact small-integer point additionFor the fixed curve $E:y^2=x^3-x+1$, choose from these known points: $P=(0,1)$, $Q=(1,1)$, $R=(-1,-1)$, $-P=(0,-1)$, or $O$. The answers use exact rational arithmetic internally for these examples.
Question 1.6For $E:y^2=x^3-x+1$, show that $(0,1)+(1,1)=(-1,-1)$.
The slope is $(1-1)/(1-0)=0$. Therefore $x_3=0-0-1=-1$ and $y_3=0(0+1)-1=-1$. The point is on $E$ since $(-1)^3-(-1)+1=1=(-1)^2$.
Question 1.7Compute $2(0,1)$ on $E:y^2=x^3-x+1$.
The tangent has slope $-1/2$.
$\lambda=(3(0)^2-1)/(2\cdot1)=-\frac12$, $x_3=\frac14$ and $y_3=\lambda(0-\frac14)-1=\frac18-1=-\frac78$. Thus $2(0,1)=(\frac14,-\frac78)$.
Question 1.8Why must a point $(r,0)$ on a nonsingular curve have order two?
Its negative is $(r,-0)=(r,0)$; therefore $P=-P$, which gives $2P=O$. Because $P\ne O$, its order is exactly two.

Points of Finite Order

Which rational points eventually return to the identity?
§2.1 Order 2 & 3§2.2 Discriminant§2.3 Integrality§2.4 Nagell–LutzQuick quiz
Chapter roadmapA rational point is called torsion if some positive multiple equals $O$. First we identify torsion of orders $2$ and $3$ geometrically and algebraically. Then we use the discriminant to control arithmetic and prove why every rational torsion point on an integral smooth Weierstrass model has integral coordinates.

§2.1 Points of Order Two and Three

Order of a pointThe order of $P$ is the least positive integer $m$ such that $mP=O$ (if such an integer exists). We also write $$E[m]=\{P\in E(\overline{\mathbb Q}):mP=O\}.$$ This is a subgroup, because the multiplication-by-$m$ map is a group homomorphism. A point of infinite order never reaches $O$ by repeated addition.
Theorem 2.1(a,b): order 2For $E:y^2=f(x)$ with $f$ a cubic having three distinct roots $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3$ over $\mathbb C$, $$2P=O\iff P=-P\iff y(P)=0$$ for $P\ne O$. Thus $$E[2]=\{O,(\alpha_1,0),(\alpha_2,0),(\alpha_3,0)\}\cong (\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^2.$$ Over $\mathbb Q$, only rational roots of $f$ yield rational nonzero 2-torsion; the rational subgroup can be trivial, $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$, or $(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^2$.
Figure 2.1: roots on the x-axis
The graph is of the polynomial $f(x)$, not the elliptic curve $y^2=f(x)$. The three root positions yield the three nonidentity 2-torsion points on $y^2=x^3-x$.
Theorem 2.1(c,d): order 3For $P\ne O$, the condition $3P=O$ is equivalent to $2P=-P$, hence to $x(2P)=x(P)$. Inserting the duplication formula produces the 3-division polynomial $$\boxed{\psi_3(x)=3x^4+4ax^3+6bx^2+12cx+4ac-b^2.}$$ Its roots are precisely the four distinct $x$-coordinates of nonidentity 3-torsion over $\mathbb C$; each gives two nonzero $y$ values. Consequently $|E[3]|=9$ and $E[3]\cong(\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z)^2$.
Why the four roots are simpleThe identity $$\psi_3=2ff''-(f')^2,\qquad \psi'_3=12f$$ shows that a multiple root of $\psi_3$ would also be a common zero of $f$ and $f'$, contradicting smoothness. Geometric interpretation: order-3 points other than $O$ are inflection points, where the tangent has triple contact with the cubic.
Question 2.1How many points of exact order three are on a smooth complex elliptic curve?
Eight. $E[3]$ has nine points, one of which is $O$ of order one. Each of the eight others must have order three.
Question 2.2For $y^2=x^3-x$, list all rational points of order two.
$f=x(x-1)(x+1)$, so they are $(-1,0),(0,0),(1,0)$. Together with $O$, they form $(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^2$.

§2.2 The Discriminant

The cubic discriminantFor the monic cubic $f(x)=x^3+ax^2+bx+c$, the book defines $$\boxed{D=a^2b^2-4b^3-4a^3c-27c^2+18abc.}$$ If the roots are $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3$, then $$D=\prod_{i<j}(\alpha_i-\alpha_j)^2.$$ The curve is nonsingular iff $D\ne0$. Chapter 4 calls a prime $p\ge3$ a prime of good reduction for this model when $p\nmid D$.
Integral equations and scalingStarting with rational coefficients, one can clear denominators using $X=d^2x$, $Y=d^3y$ with a suitable positive integer $d$. Then $$Y^2=X^3+(d^2a)X^2+(d^4b)X+d^6c.$$ The new coefficients are integers. This is why Nagell–Lutz is formulated for an integral Weierstrass equation, rather than an arbitrary rational model.
Why the discriminant enters torsion calculationsThe tangent slope is $f'(x)/(2y)$. If both $P=(x,y)$ and $2P$ are integral and $y\ne0$, constraints on the duplication formula and resultant identities force divisibility of a fixed integer built from the coefficients. The book shows that $y\mid D$; a sharper form $y^2\mid D$ is mentioned and left as an exercise. This is only a necessary condition for torsion.
Question 2.4Compute the discriminant of $f(x)=x^3-x^2+x$. Is the curve smooth?
Here $a=-1,b=1,c=0$. Thus $D=a^2b^2-4b^3=1-4=-3$. Since $D\neq0$, the curve is smooth over $\mathbb Q$. Its reductions at $p=3$ are singular.

§2.3 Points of Finite Order Have Integer Coordinates

The arithmetic challengeElliptic addition of rational points can introduce arbitrarily large denominators. The theorem asserts that this cannot happen for a torsion point on a smooth integral Weierstrass equation: every affine rational torsion point has both coordinates in $\mathbb Z$. The book proves this with a prime-by-prime analysis of denominators.
$p$-adic order and the local ringFor a rational $r\ne0$ and prime $p$, let $v_p(r)$ be the exponent of $p$ in the numerator minus its exponent in the denominator. Set $v_p(0)=+\infty$. The ring $$R_p=\{r\in\mathbb Q:v_p(r)\ge0\}$$ consists of rationals with denominators not divisible by $p$. If $P=(x,y)$ on an integral nonsingular cubic has negative $p$-adic valuation of $x$, then the curve equation forces $$v_p(x)=-2\nu,\quad v_p(y)=-3\nu\quad(\nu\ge1).$$ This even-square / odd-cube denominator pattern reappears in Chapter 3.
A better coordinate near infinityInstead of $x,y$, use $t=x/y$ and $s=1/y$ near $O$. The equation becomes $$s=t^3+at^2s+bts^2+cs^3.$$ For $\nu\ge1$, define $$E(p^\nu)=\{O\}\cup\{(x,y)\in E(\mathbb Q):v_p(x)\le-2\nu,\ v_p(y)\le-3\nu\}.$$ These are the small neighbourhood subgroups near $O$. On this local branch, $v_p(t)\ge\nu$ and $v_p(s)=3v_p(t)$; the condition on $t$ alone does not characterize the subgroup. Set $t(O)=s(O)=0$. By rewriting the chord-and-tangent law in these coordinates, the book proves that $$t(P+Q)\equiv t(P)+t(Q)\pmod{p^{3\nu}R_p}$$ whenever $P,Q\in E(p^\nu)$. That approximate additivity is the arithmetic key.
Corollary 2.4 (no torsion near infinity)For every prime $p$, the subgroup $E(p)$ contains no nonidentity torsion point. Proof idea: if $E(p)$ contained nonzero torsion, a multiple would have prime order $\ell$. Write $\nu=v_p(t(P))\ge1$. Repeated addition gives $t(\ell P)\equiv\ell t(P)\pmod{p^{3\nu}R_p}$. But $v_p(\ell t(P))$ is $\nu$ when $\ell\ne p$ and $\nu+1$ when $\ell=p$, both less than $3\nu$. Thus $\ell P$ cannot equal $O$, a contradiction. Therefore a rational torsion point has no prime in its coordinate denominators, and so both $x,y$ are integers.
Question 2.5If $P=(m/e^2,n/e^3)$ is a rational torsion point with $m,n,e\in\mathbb Z$, $e>0$ and suitable coprimality, what must $e$ equal?
$e=1$. If a prime divided $e$, then the point would lie in the small $p$-adic subgroup near infinity, which the book proves is torsion-free.

§2.4 The Nagell–Lutz Theorem and Further Developments

Theorem 2.5 (Nagell–Lutz, as stated in the book)Let $$E:\ y^2=x^3+ax^2+bx+c,\qquad a,b,c\in\mathbb Z,$$ be nonsingular and let $D$ be its cubic discriminant. If $(x,y)\in E(\mathbb Q)$ has finite order, then $$\boxed{x,y\in\mathbb Z\quad\text{and}\quad[y=0\ \text{or}\ y\mid D].}$$ The point $O$ is also torsion. The book also notes the stronger necessary condition $y^2\mid D$ when $y\ne0$.
One-way theorem: do not reverse itIf an integral point satisfies $y\mid D$, that does not prove it has finite order. It merely survives a filter. To establish torsion, compute multiples until some $nP=O$. To establish infinite order, it suffices to find a multiple $nP$ with a nonintegral coordinate, because all multiples of a torsion point would themselves be torsion and integral.
Example: a point of order fourConsider $$E:\ y^2=x^3-x^2+x,\quad D=-3.$$ The point $P=(1,1)$ lies on $E$. The tangent slope is $\lambda=(3-2+1)/(2)=1$, so $2P=(0,0)$. The point $(0,0)$ has order two; hence $P$ has order four, and $3P=-P=(1,-1)$. The rational torsion subgroup for this example is cyclic of order four, as established in the book.
Interactive lab · Nagell–Lutz necessary candidatesTest integral points on a small curve $y^2=x^3+ax^2+bx+c$. The lab searches $-25\le x\le25$, computes the discriminant, and shows points meeting the necessary $y=0$ or $y\mid D$ test. Passing the filter is not a torsion proof.
Question 2.6Suppose $P\in E(\mathbb Q)$ and $x(2P)=137/64$. Can $P$ be torsion on a nonsingular integer Weierstrass equation?
No. If $P$ were torsion, so would $2P$ be, and by Nagell–Lutz the $x$-coordinate of $2P$ would be an integer. It is not.
Question 2.7Why does Nagell–Lutz imply that $E(\mathbb Q)_{\mathrm{tors}}$ is finite?
For $y\ne0$, list the divisors of a fixed integer $D$.
There are finitely many integers $y$ dividing nonzero $D$, plus $y=0$. Each fixed $y$ imposes the cubic equation $f(x)=y^2$, which has at most three integer roots. Add the identity $O$: finitely many candidates, so finitely many torsion points.
Question 2.8For $y^2=x^3-x^2+x$, why is $(1,1)$ not of order two despite its integer coordinates?
A nonidentity point has order two exactly when $y=0$. Here $y=1$. Direct addition shows $2(1,1)=(0,0)$, hence $(1,1)$ has order four.
Theorem 2.7: Mazur’s classificationFor an elliptic curve over $\mathbb Q$, the rational torsion subgroup is one of $$\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z\quad(1\le N\le10\text{ or }N=12),$$ or $$\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\oplus\mathbb Z/(2N)\mathbb Z\quad(1\le N\le4).$$ Thus a rational torsion point can have order $1,2,\ldots,10$ or $12$, but not $11$. The book states this deep theorem without proof; Nagell–Lutz alone does not give this classification.

The Group of Rational Points

§3.1 Descent§3.2 Translation§3.3 Doubling§3.4 Isogenies§3.5 Mordell§3.6 Rank§3.7 SingularQuiz
Chapter roadmapThis chapter addresses the central question: why are finitely many rational points enough to generate all rational points? The height measures how complicated a rational coordinate is; descent uses an index-finite doubling map to replace a point with a smaller one. The authors develop explicit 2-isogenies to prove the crucial finite-index statement in the important special case of a rational point of order two.

§3.1 Heights and Descent

Height of rational numbers and pointsWrite $r=m/n\in\mathbb Q$ in lowest terms with $n>0$. Define $$H(r)=\max\{|m|,|n|\},\qquad h(r)=\log H(r).$$ For an affine point $P=(x,y)\in E(\mathbb Q)$ let $$\boxed{H(P)=H(x),\qquad h(P)=\log H(x).}$$ Set $H(O)=1$, so $h(O)=0$. Logarithms turn approximate multiplication of sizes into addition.
Finiteness of bounded-height sets · Lemma 3.1There are only finitely many rationals $m/n$ with $H(m/n)\le M$, because there are only finitely many possible numerators and denominators. For a fixed rational $x$ on $E$, at most two $y$ values satisfy $y^2=f(x)$. Therefore $$\#\{P\in E(\mathbb Q):h(P)\le B\}<\infty$$ for every finite real $B$.
Interactive lab · why absolute value misses complexityCompare $1/2$ with $20000/9999$: their numerical sizes may be similar, yet their heights are very different. Enter a numerator and denominator to compute the reduced height.
Four inputs to the descent theorem · Lemmas 3.1–3.4For an abelian group $\Gamma=E(\mathbb Q)$ equipped with $h$, the book isolates:
  1. Bounded-height finiteness: only finitely many $P$ satisfy $h(P)\le M$.
  2. Translation estimate: for fixed $P_0$ there is $\kappa_0$ with $h(P+P_0)\le2h(P)+\kappa_0$.
  3. Doubling estimate: there is $\kappa$ with $h(2P)\ge4h(P)-\kappa$.
  4. Finite quotient: $[\Gamma:2\Gamma]<\infty$.
The first is immediate; the next two are proved by rational-function height estimates; the fourth is arithmetically deepest.
Theorem 3.5: abstract Descent TheoremAny abelian group with a nonnegative height satisfying all four properties above is finitely generated. Choose finitely many coset representatives $Q_1,\dots,Q_s$ of $2\Gamma$. For any $P$, write $$P=Q_{i_1}+2P_1,\quad P_1=Q_{i_2}+2P_2,\quad\ldots.$$ The height estimates yield $$h(P_j)\le\frac12h(P_{j-1})+C.$$ Once the previous height is large enough, this gives geometric decrease, for instance $h(P_j)\le\tfrac34h(P_{j-1})$. Ultimately $P_m$ lies in a finite bounded-height set. Reversing the equations expresses $P$ as a combination of the finite coset representatives and those final points.

Question 3.1Why does $|x|\le 2$ fail to give a finite set of rational numbers, while $H(x)\le2$ does?
There are infinitely many rationals in $[-2,2]$ (such as $1/n$). But a reduced fraction with $H(x)\le2$ has numerator and denominator drawn from a finite set of integers, so only finitely many values occur.
Question 3.2Why are the finitely many coset representatives of $2\Gamma$ essential to the descent proof?
They bound the translation constants uniformly.
Each $P$ can be written $P=Q_i+2P_1$. Because there are finitely many $Q_i$, a single constant controls all translations by $-Q_i$. This gives a uniform height reduction and ensures the generating set assembled at the end is finite.

§3.2 The Height of $P+P_0$

Denominators have special shapesFor an integer-coefficient Weierstrass equation and an affine rational point one can write, in suitably reduced form, $$\boxed{x=\frac m{e^2},\qquad y=\frac n{e^3}}$$ with integers $m,n,e$ and $e>0$. Substitution into $y^2=x^3+ax^2+bx+c$ and comparison of prime valuations explain why the denominator exponents are even for $x$ and multiples of three for $y$.
Lemma 3.2: translation increases height at most quadraticallyFor each fixed $P_0\in E(\mathbb Q)$, there exists a constant $\kappa_0$ such that $$\boxed{h(P+P_0)\le2h(P)+\kappa_0}$$ for all $P\in E(\mathbb Q)$. The constant may depend on $P_0$ and the curve but not on $P$.
Proof mechanismFor $P=(x,y)$ and fixed $P_0=(x_0,y_0)$, $$x(P+P_0)=\left(\frac{y-y_0}{x-x_0}\right)^2-a-x-x_0.$$ Substitute the curve equation to eliminate $y^2$, then use $x=m/e^2$, $y=n/e^3$ and clear denominators. Numerator and denominator can be bounded by a constant times a suitable square of $H(P)$; taking logs gives the estimate. Exceptional points $O,\pm P_0$ form a finite set and can be absorbed into $\kappa_0$.
Question 3.3Does Lemma 3.2 say $h(P+P_0)\le2h(P)$ for every curve and every $P_0$?
No. The additive constant $\kappa_0$ is essential; it accounts for the fixed point and coefficients. The statement is a uniform upper bound with that constant.

§3.3 The Height of $2P$

Lemma 3.3: doubling expands logarithmic heightThere is a curve-dependent $\kappa$ such that $$\boxed{h(2P)\ge4h(P)-\kappa}$$ for all $P\in E(\mathbb Q)$. Compare this lower bound with the translation upper bound: together, when $2P_j=P_{j-1}-Q_i$, they force $h(P_j)$ to shrink for large heights.
Why a fourth power appearsThe duplication formula gives the rational map $$x\longmapsto x(2P)=\frac{\phi(x)}{\psi(x)},\qquad\begin{cases}\phi(x)=x^4-2bx^2-8cx+b^2-4ac,\\\psi(x)=4x^3+4ax^2+4bx+4c.\end{cases}$$ The numerator and denominator are homogeneous degree-four expressions after writing $x=m/n$. Unless cancellation were uncontrolled, $H(x(2P))$ should have size comparable to $H(x)^4$. The book proves the needed bound using the fact that the numerator and denominator polynomials have no common root for a nonsingular cubic, a resultant/Bézout identity that bounds common factors, and a compactness argument for the archimedean size.
A warning about intuitionHeight does not necessarily increase under every individual doubling. The theorem has a constant error term $\kappa$ and becomes useful for large height. It is not claiming that the raw $x$-coordinate, or even $H(P)$, strictly increases at every step.
Question 3.4Combine $h(2Q)\ge4h(Q)-\kappa$ and $h(P-R)\le2h(P)+\kappa'$ when $2Q=P-R$. What follows?
$$\begin{aligned}4h(Q)-\kappa&\le h(2Q)=h(P-R)\\&\le2h(P)+\kappa'.\end{aligned}$$ Thus $$h(Q)\le\frac12h(P)+\frac{\kappa+\kappa'}4.$$ For large $h(P)$ this makes the new point smaller.

§3.4 A Useful Homomorphism

Why the book assumes rational 2-torsion hereThe finite-index statement $[E(\mathbb Q):2E(\mathbb Q)]<\infty$ is the hardest input. To prove it with elementary arithmetic, the authors now suppose $f(x)$ has a rational root, translate it to $x=0$, and obtain $$E:\ y^2=x^3+ax^2+bx=x(x^2+ax+b).$$ The point $T=(0,0)$ then has order two. The full general Mordell theorem requires ideas beyond this elementary special-case proof.
A companion curve and 2-isogenyDefine $$\boxed{E':\ y^2=x^3+a'x^2+b'x},\quad a'=-2a,\ b'=a^2-4b.$$ The map $$\boxed{\varphi(x,y)=\left(\frac{y^2}{x^2},\frac{y(x^2-b)}{x^2}\right)}$$ sends points on $E$ to points on $E'$ for $x\ne0$, with $\varphi(O)=\varphi(T)=O$. The book checks algebraically that it is a homomorphism and that $\ker\varphi=\{O,T\}$.
The dual map and the doubling factorizationApplying the same construction to $E'$ and then rescaling coordinates gives a homomorphism $\psi:E'\to E$ such that $$\boxed{\psi\circ\varphi=[2]\quad\text{on }E,\qquad\varphi\circ\psi=[2]\quad\text{on }E'.}$$ Here $[2]$ means $P\mapsto2P$. Thus multiplication by two factors through two degree-two isogenies. Both kernels contain two points over $\mathbb C$.
The reason to factor the doubling mapTo show $2E(\mathbb Q)$ has finite index, it is enough to show that the images of $E(\mathbb Q)$ and $E'(\mathbb Q)$ under these two isogenies have finite index. Such image questions can be translated into concrete tests for whether the $x$-coordinate is a square in $\mathbb Q$. This makes the arithmetic tractable.
Question 3.5On $E:y^2=x^3+ax^2+bx$, why is $(0,0)$ a 2-torsion point?
It lies on $E$ because $f(0)=0$, and it equals its own negative because its $y$-coordinate is zero. So $2(0,0)=O$. Smoothness requires $b(a^2-4b)\ne0$.
Question 3.6Use the isogeny formula to determine the $x$-coordinate of $\varphi(P)$ for $P=(x,y)$ with $x\ne0$. Why is it special?
The first coordinate is $y^2/x^2=(y/x)^2$, a rational square. This is the basic clue behind the square-class descent map and the description of the rational image of $\varphi$.

§3.5 Mordell's Theorem

The square-class groupLet $\mathbb Q^*/\mathbb Q^{*2}$ be the multiplicative group of nonzero rational numbers modulo squares. Two numbers are equivalent if their ratio is a rational square. Every class has a squarefree integer representative, including the sign. The book defines an arithmetic map, for $E:y^2=x^3+ax^2+bx$, $$\alpha:E(\mathbb Q)\to\mathbb Q^*/\mathbb Q^{*2},\quad\alpha(O)=1,\quad\alpha((0,0))=b,\quad\alpha((x,y))=[x]\ (x\ne0).$$ It is a group homomorphism, with kernel the image of the dual isogeny $\psi(E'(\mathbb Q))$.
Why only finitely many square classes appearWrite $x=m/e^2$, $y=n/e^3$ in lowest terms. The equation becomes $$n^2=m(m^2+ame^2+be^4).$$ Any prime dividing $m$ to an odd power must divide $b$, because the common divisors of $m$ and the second factor are supported on primes dividing $b$. Thus if $p_1,\dots,p_t$ are the distinct prime divisors of $b$, every image class has a representative of the form $$\pm p_1^{\epsilon_1}\cdots p_t^{\epsilon_t},\quad \epsilon_i\in\{0,1\}.$$ There are at most $2^{t+1}$ such classes. Hence $E(\mathbb Q)/\psi(E'(\mathbb Q))$ is finite; applying the same argument to $E'$ gives finiteness for the other isogeny quotient.
Theorem 3.10 (the version actually proved in the book)If $E:y^2=x^3+ax^2+bx$ is nonsingular with $a,b\in\mathbb Z$ (and hence has a rational 2-torsion point), then $E(\mathbb Q)$ is a finitely generated abelian group. Logic: the two finite-index isogeny images imply $[E(\mathbb Q):2E(\mathbb Q)]<\infty$. The three height properties from §§3.1–3.3 and the abstract descent theorem then finish the proof. The general Mordell theorem holds for all elliptic curves over $\mathbb Q$; the book emphasizes that its elementary proof is restricted to this special case.
Question 3.7Let $b=30$ in $y^2=x^3+ax^2+bx$. What is the largest possible number of square classes in the image of $\alpha$ from the elementary bound?
There are three distinct prime divisors of $30$, namely $2,3,5$. The possible representatives are $\pm2^{\epsilon_1}3^{\epsilon_2}5^{\epsilon_3}$, at most $2^{3+1}=16$ square classes. The actual image can be smaller.
Question 3.8Is proving $[E(\mathbb Q):2E(\mathbb Q)]<\infty$ by itself enough to prove finite generation?
Not in complete generality for arbitrary abelian groups. In the argument here, finite index is combined with the existence of a height satisfying bounded-height finiteness and the two growth estimates; the Descent Theorem uses all four ingredients.

§3.6 Examples and Further Developments

Rank, torsion and the structure theoremFinite generation permits a decomposition $$\boxed{E(\mathbb Q)\cong\mathbb Z^r\oplus E(\mathbb Q)_{\mathrm{tors}}.}$$ The nonnegative integer $r$ is the rank, the number of independent infinite-order generators. $r=0$ means the rational group is finite. In general, determining $r$ is much harder than identifying torsion.
A formula for the 2-descent rankFor a finitely generated group, $$[E(\mathbb Q):2E(\mathbb Q)]=2^r\,|E(\mathbb Q)[2]|.$$ For the pair of 2-isogenous curves $E$ and $E'$ in this chapter, the book derives $$\boxed{2^r=\frac{|\alpha(E(\mathbb Q))|\,|\alpha'(E'(\mathbb Q))|}{4}.}$$ Both square-class images are finite. The hard part is deciding which candidate square classes actually come from rational points.
The quartic equations used in practiceChoose a nonzero squarefree integer divisor $b_1$ of $b$, allowing either sign, and set $b_2=b/b_1$. A candidate class $[b_1]$ arising from a rational point leads to an integer solution of a quartic equation of the form $$\boxed{N^2=b_1M^4+aM^2e^2+b_2e^4},\qquad b=b_1b_2.$$ For an integer solution with $M\ne0$, $e\ne0$ and $\gcd(M,e)=1$, rational points recover as $x=b_1M^2/e^2$, $y=b_1MN/e^3$. For particular candidates, congruences or sign arguments may rule out solutions; displaying such an admissible solution confirms $[b_1]$ because $x=b_1(M/e)^2\ne0$. The zero solution does not count. The exceptional image classes $[1]=\alpha(O)$ and $[b]=\alpha((0,0))$ are included directly; the displayed recovery formulas are not used when $e=0$. The general existence question for such quartics is difficult, so this is a tool rather than a guaranteed rank algorithm.
Book example: rank zero via a 2-isogenyThe book studies the paired curves $$E:\ y^2=x^3-x,\qquad E':\ y^2=x^3+4x.$$ For $E$, the classes $+1$ and $-1$ already occur via $O$ and $(0,0)$; on the companion curve, a small collection of candidate classes must be checked. This illustrates how explicit square-class tests can establish the rank. On $E(\mathbb Q)$, the four points $O,(-1,0),(0,0),(1,0)$ are torsion; no free part occurs.
Why high rank is difficultEven though the Mordell–Weil group is finitely generated, the smallest useful generators can have enormous coordinates. The examples and historical rank records in §3.6 are explicitly time-dependent snapshots from the 2015 edition, not present-day records. The underlying lesson is timeless: finite generation does not yield a universally efficient method to find generators or rank.
Question 3.9If $E(\mathbb Q)\cong\mathbb Z^2\oplus\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$, what is its rank and how many torsion points does it have?
Rank $r=2$ (two copies of $\mathbb Z$). Its torsion subgroup is $\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$, with four points.
Question 3.10For the same group $\mathbb Z^2\oplus\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$, determine $|\Gamma/2\Gamma|$.
$\Gamma[2]$ has two elements, so $|\Gamma/2\Gamma|=2^r|\Gamma[2]|=2^2\cdot2=8$.

§3.7 Singular Cubic Curves

The reason smoothness mattersAn affine singular point lies on the curve and has both partial derivatives of its defining equation equal to zero. An irreducible plane cubic over an algebraically closed field cannot have two distinct singular points: a line joining them would meet the cubic with total multiplicity at least four, exceeding its degree three by Bézout’s theorem; irreducibility ensures that the line is not a component. For the singular Weierstrass cubics below, one can still define a chord-and-tangent group on its nonsingular points, but the group looks very different from the rational point group on an elliptic curve.
Theorem 3.15: node versus cuspThe chapter explicitly identifies two groups:
  • For the nodal cubic $C:y^2=x^3+x^2$, the nonsingular rational points form a group isomorphic to $(\mathbb Q^*,\times)$ via $$\varphi(x,y)=\frac{y-x}{y+x},\qquad \varphi(O)=1.$$
  • For the cuspidal cubic $C:y^2=x^3$, the nonsingular rational points form a group isomorphic to $(\mathbb Q,+)$ via $$\varphi(x,y)=\frac{x}{y},\qquad\varphi(O)=0.$$
Both $\mathbb Q^*$ and $\mathbb Q$ are not finitely generated, illustrating why the nonsingularity hypothesis in Mordell's theorem is indispensable.
A direct parametrization for a cuspOn $y^2=x^3$, points $(t^2,t^3)$ lie on the curve for any $t\in\mathbb Q$. Apart from the cusp $(0,0)$, these account for the finite nonsingular rational points; the point at infinity is the group identity. In the homomorphism above, $x/y=1/t$, so rational addition on the cuspidal cubic becomes the ordinary addition of this parameter.
Question 3.11Why does the group $(\mathbb Q,+)$ fail to be finitely generated?
Given finitely many rational generators, choose a common denominator $N$. Every integer linear combination lies in $(1/N)\mathbb Z$. But $1/(N+1)$ is not generally in that subgroup, and choosing a prime larger than all denominator factors makes this contradiction clear. No finite set generates all rationals additively.
Question 3.12What breaks if you try to apply the descent theorem directly to $y^2=x^3$?
The singular curve does not have the same nonsingular elliptic-curve group structure. Its smooth rational group is isomorphic to $(\mathbb Q,+)$, which is not finitely generated; therefore it cannot satisfy all the descent-theorem hypotheses together.

Cubic Curves over Finite Fields

Point counts, reduction, factoring integers and elliptic-curve cryptography.
§4.1 Finite fields§4.2 Gauss§4.3 Reduction§4.4 Factoring§4.5 CryptographyQuick quiz
Chapter roadmapWe replace rational coordinates by residues modulo a prime $p$. The same chord-and-tangent formulas now define a finite abelian group that we can enumerate. The number of points is controlled by Hasse's bound; the book proves Gauss's exact result for a special cubic, uses reduction to constrain rational torsion, and develops two surprising applications: Lenstra's factorization algorithm and elliptic-curve cryptography.

§4.1 Rational Points over Finite Fields

Finite fields and the same group lawFor a prime $p$, $\mathbb F_p=\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$. Consider $$E:\ y^2=x^3+ax^2+bx+c\pmod p,\quad a,b,c\in\mathbb F_p.$$ For $p\ne2$ and $D\not\equiv0\pmod p$, its $\mathbb F_p$-points and $O$ form a finite abelian group $E(\mathbb F_p)$. The formulas are exactly those from §1.4, but division means multiplying by a modular inverse; nonzero elements are invertible because $\mathbb F_p$ is a field.
Finding all points by enumerationFor each $x\in\{0,\ldots,p-1\}$ calculate $r=f(x)\pmod p$. If $r=0$, exactly one $y$ solves $y^2=r$; if $r$ is a nonzero quadratic residue, there are two $y$; otherwise none. Finally add $O$. In symbols, with the Legendre symbol $\chi$, $$\#E(\mathbb F_p)=p+1+\sum_{x\in\mathbb F_p}\chi(f(x)),$$ where $\chi(0)=0$. This also motivates the expected size near $p+1$.
Theorem 4.1: Hasse–Weil boundFor a smooth elliptic curve over $\mathbb F_p$, $$\boxed{\left|\#E(\mathbb F_p)-(p+1)\right|\le2\sqrt p.}$$ The book states the more general genus-$g$ bound $|N_p-(p+1)|\le2g\sqrt p$ for smooth projective geometrically irreducible curves of genus $g$, with $N_p$ counting all projective points; elliptic curves have genus $1$. The proof is not supplied in §4.1.
Book example over $\mathbb F_5$On $E:y^2=x^3+x+1\pmod5$, $$E(\mathbb F_5)=\{O,(0,\pm1),(2,\pm1),(3,\pm1),(4,\pm2)\}.$$ So $\#E(\mathbb F_5)=9$. Taking $P=(0,1)$, the multiples run through all nine elements; therefore this group is cyclic of order $9$. In particular, $2P=(4,2)$ and $3P=(2,1)$.
Interactive lab · enumerate a finite elliptic curveEnter an odd prime $p\le101$ and integer $a,b,c$. The tool checks smoothness, enumerates the $p$-points, tests Hasse's interval and lets you compute $nP$. This is a learning tool, not a symbolic algebra system.
Question 4.1How many points does $y^2=x^3+x+1$ have over $\mathbb F_5$, including $O$?
Nine: there are two points for each $x=0,2,3,4$, none for $x=1$, and one point at infinity. Hence $2+2+2+2+1=9$.
Question 4.2Hasse says $\#E(\mathbb F_7)$ is an integer in what interval?
$7+1-2\sqrt7\le N\le7+1+2\sqrt7$, i.e. approximately $2.71\le N\le13.29$. Thus $3\le N\le13$ (with additional restrictions possible for a specific curve).

§4.2 A Theorem of Gauss

The special Fermat cubic and projective pointsThe book takes the cubic $$X^3+Y^3+Z^3=0$$ and counts nonzero triples $(X,Y,Z)\in\mathbb F_p^3$ up to multiplication by a common nonzero scalar. Call the number of these projective points $M_p$. For $p\ne3$, this is a smooth plane cubic; the distinction between affine points and points at infinity is important for the count.
Theorem 4.2 (Gauss), exact point count
  • If $p\not\equiv1\pmod3$, then $$\boxed{M_p=p+1.}$$
  • If $p\equiv1\pmod3$, write $$4p=A^2+27B^2$$ with integers $A,B$, choosing the sign of $A$ such that $A\equiv1\pmod3$. Then $$\boxed{M_p=p+1+A.}$$
For $p\equiv1\pmod3$, $|A|<2\sqrt p$, in agreement with Hasse's bound. The formula is a classical special case, not a recipe for all elliptic curves.
Proof idea when $p\not\equiv1\pmod3$The multiplicative group $\mathbb F_p^*$ is cyclic of order $p-1$. If $3\nmid(p-1)$, cubing is a bijection on $\mathbb F_p$. So the projective Fermat cubic is equivalent, by $U=X^3,V=Y^3,W=Z^3$, to the projective line $U+V+W=0$, which has exactly $p+1$ points.
Proof idea when $p\equiv1\pmod3$Now cubing has kernel of size three on $\mathbb F_p^*$. The nonzero residues split into three cosets: cubic residues $R$ and two other classes $S,T$. The book counts triples with $x+y+z=0$ according to these cosets and introduces Gaussian periods, sums of $p$th roots of unity indexed by $R,S,T$. Relations among those period sums determine integers $A,B$ with $4p=A^2+27B^2$ and give $M_p=p+1+A$. This is a substantial number-theoretic argument, not merely an application of Hasse's estimate.
Interactive lab · Gauss versus direct countingChoose a prime $2\le p\le101$ and compare the Gauss formula with an independent projective count. For $p=3$ the displayed identity still holds, although the cubic is singular in characteristic three.
Question 4.3For $p=7$, find $A,B$ with $4p=A^2+27B^2$, $A\equiv1\pmod3$, then give $M_7$.
$28=1^2+27(1)^2$. Choose $A=1$, $B=\pm1$; then $M_7=7+1+1=9$.
Question 4.4For $p=13$, $4p=52=25+27$. Which sign of $A=\pm5$ satisfies the condition, and what is $M_{13}$?
$-5\equiv1\pmod3$. Thus $A=-5$ and $M_{13}=13+1-5=9$.
Beyond Gauss: modularityFor a general elliptic curve $E/\mathbb Q$, exact counts as $p$ varies are more complicated. The modularity theorem relates $a_p=p+1-\#E(\mathbb F_p)$ at primes of good reduction to Fourier coefficients of a weight-two modular form. The book closes §4.2 by explaining this connection and its role in Fermat’s Last Theorem; it does not prove modularity here.

§4.3 Points of Finite Order Revisited

Reduction of an integral model modulo $p$Let $E:y^2=x^3+ax^2+bx+c$ with integer coefficients and discriminant $D\ne0$. Reducing all coefficients mod $p$ gives $\widetilde E$ over $\mathbb F_p$. When $p\nmid2D$, the reduction is smooth, and the group law works mod $p$. The torsion subgroup $\Phi=E(\mathbb Q)_{\mathrm{tors}}$ has integral affine coordinates by Nagell–Lutz, so its points can be reduced modulo such primes.
Theorem 4.4: reduction is injective on torsionFor this integral Weierstrass model and any prime $p\nmid2D$, $$\boxed{\Phi\hookrightarrow\widetilde E(\mathbb F_p)}$$ is a group embedding. The proof checks compatibility with addition and observes that no nonidentity integral torsion point reduces to $O$; therefore the reduction homomorphism has trivial kernel on $\Phi$. In particular, $$\boxed{|\Phi|\mid\#\widetilde E(\mathbb F_p).}$$ Using two or more good primes often sharply restricts the size of rational torsion.
Book example: $y^2=x^3+3$Here $D=-243$, so both $p=5$ and $p=7$ are primes of good reduction. The book finds $$\#E(\mathbb F_5)=6,\qquad \#E(\mathbb F_7)=13.$$ Therefore the torsion order divides $\gcd(6,13)=1$. Hence $E(\mathbb Q)$ has no nonidentity torsion. In particular $(1,2)$ is an infinite-order rational point, since it lies on this curve.
Book example: $y^2=x^3+x$Here $D=-4$. Over $\mathbb F_3$ the group has four elements and is cyclic; over $\mathbb F_5$ it has four elements and is isomorphic to $(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^2$. Their possible common subgroup structure, together with the evident point $(0,0)$, forces rational torsion to be precisely $\{O,(0,0)\}$.
Question 4.5Suppose an elliptic curve has good reduction at primes $p,q$ with $\#E(\mathbb F_p)=12$ and $\#E(\mathbb F_q)=15$. What can you conclude about the rational torsion order?
The torsion order divides both $12$ and $15$, hence divides $\gcd(12,15)=3$. So it is either $1$ or $3$; a rational 3-torsion point would settle the case.
Question 4.6For $E:y^2=x^3+3$, why does counting points modulo only one good prime not immediately prove torsion is trivial?
A count of $6$ at $p=5$ permits rational torsion orders $1,2,3,6$. The additional count $13$ at $p=7$ forces the order to divide both and hence be $1$.

§4.4 A Factorization Algorithm Using Elliptic Curves

Fast powering and greatest common divisorsBefore Lenstra's method, the book develops repeated squaring to compute $a^k\bmod n$ efficiently, and the Euclidean algorithm for $\gcd(u,v)$. Both require only a number of elementary steps proportional to the logarithm of the input sizes. Pollard's $p-1$ method is the conceptual predecessor: it seeks a prime divisor $p\mid n$ for which $p-1$ divides a chosen highly composite exponent $k$.
Why a modular elliptic-curve computation can reveal a factorOver a prime field every nonzero denominator has an inverse. Over $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ with composite $n$, it need not. To add points, one must invert denominators such as $x_2-x_1$ or $2y_1$. For a proposed denominator $d$, compute $g=\gcd(d,n)$:
  • $g=1$: the modular inverse exists; continue.
  • $1<g<n$: $g$ is a nontrivial factor of $n$; success.
  • $g=n$: this attempt did not isolate a factor; choose another curve or step.
A failed modular inverse reveals a proper factor only when $1<\gcd(d,n)<n$; a gcd equal to $n$ is inconclusive.
Lenstra's elliptic-curve factorization (ECM), simplified workflowChoose a curve $E:y^2=x^3+bx+c\pmod n$ and a point $P=(x_0,y_0)$ by selecting $b,x_0,y_0$ and defining $c=y_0^2-x_0^3-bx_0\pmod n$. First check $\gcd(2D,n)$ for this model: a proper gcd gives a factor, while a gcd equal to $n$ calls for a new choice (or handling the factor $2$ separately). If the gcd is $1$, the curve is smooth modulo each prime factor of $n$. Compute a large scalar multiple $kP$ efficiently using double-and-add, often building $k$ from small-prime factors or factorials. At each attempted modular inversion check $\gcd(d,n)$. If a nontrivial gcd appears, report it. Otherwise change the bound or curve and try again. Varying curves changes the group orders modulo the unknown prime factors of $n$, the key advantage over fixed-group $p-1$ factoring.
Book's numerical factorization exampleThe book factors $$n=1\,715\,761\,513=26\,927\cdot63\,719$$ using the curve $y^2=x^3+5x-9\pmod n$ and a point built during the algorithm. A particular attempted addition needs the inverse of $$d=-456\,520\,358\pmod n.$$ But $$\boxed{\gcd(d,n)=26\,927,}$$ which exposes one prime factor of $n$ without first knowing the factorization.
Interactive lab · gcd from a failed inversionTry the book's numbers or replace them with your own integers. This demonstrates exactly the arithmetic event ECM exploits; it is not a complete high-performance factorization program.
Question 4.7Why can addition formulas be used directly in $\mathbb F_p$ but not always in $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ for composite $n$?
All nonzero elements of the field $\mathbb F_p$ are invertible. For composite $n$, a nonzero residue can share a nontrivial gcd with $n$ and thus have no inverse. ECM detects such a denominator.
Question 4.8What is the practical benefit of trying a different curve when an ECM attempt fails?
For a fixed unknown prime divisor $p$ of $n$, $|E(\mathbb F_p)|$ varies when $E$ varies. A new curve may have group order built from small primes, making a scalar multiple annihilate a point modulo that $p$ and forcing an unsuccessful modular inverse that reveals a factor.

§4.5 Elliptic Curve Cryptography

The elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem (ECDLP)Let $E(\mathbb F_p)$ be a finite elliptic-curve group with a specified nonidentity point $P$ and $Q\in\langle P\rangle$. The ECDLP asks: $$\boxed{\text{Find an integer }m\text{ such that }Q=mP.}$$ The answer $m$ is determined modulo the order of $P$. Scalar multiplication with a known $m$ is computationally easy by repeated doubling. Recovering $m$ from $P,Q$ is expected to be hard for carefully chosen cryptographic groups, which is the mathematical basis for many elliptic-curve protocols.
Book example: a small ECDLPOn $E:y^2=x^3+x^2+x+1$ over $\mathbb F_{97}$, the book takes $$P=(7,20),\qquad Q=(17,46).$$ It finds $\boxed{47P=Q}$. Rather than testing every $m$, a collision search compares a list of multiples $aP$ with a list of $Q-bP$ and uses any matching point to recover $m=a+b$. This motivates roughly square-root-sized search algorithms for the small example.
Elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman (ECDH), conceptual constructionChoose a publicly known point $P$ in a suitable large subgroup. Alice chooses secret $a$ and publishes $A=aP$; Bob chooses secret $b$ and publishes $B=bP$. Then $$\boxed{aB=abP=bA.}$$ The shared point can be incorporated into a secure key-derivation protocol. Security caution: authenticating participants, validating points, using approved curves, fresh secrets and proper KDFs are essential in actual cryptography; the simple group formula alone does not provide an implementable secure protocol.
Interactive lab · toy ECDH with the book's $\mathbb F_5$ curveUse $E:y^2=x^3+x+1\pmod5$ and $P=(0,1)$, of order $9$. Pick toy secrets $a,b\in\{1,\dots,8\}$. The tool computes public points and the common secret point. Educational only: this tiny curve provides no security.
Why elliptic curves attracted cryptographersThe book compares elliptic-curve discrete logarithms with the logarithm problem in multiplicative finite fields, explaining the potential to achieve comparable practical security with much shorter group elements. It also stresses a vital limitation: hardness assertions for factoring and discrete logarithms are not proven in the strongest complexity-theoretic sense, and small examples like this course's widgets are trivially breakable.
Question 4.9With $P=(0,1)$ of order $9$, what is the relation between $a(bP)$ and $b(aP)$?
Both equal $(ab)P$, by associativity of the group law. This equality underpins the arithmetic part of elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman.
Question 4.10If repeated doubling computes $mP$ quickly, why does that not immediately make the ECDLP easy?
Forward scalar multiplication only needs additions using a known $m$. Reversing it requires discovering $m$ from $P$ and $mP$; there is no known comparably fast method for properly chosen large elliptic-curve groups.
