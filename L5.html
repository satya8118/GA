Seven Lectures on Field Extensions

Lecture 5
Ruler and Compass Constructions

Geometry meets fields: why some ancient problems are impossible.
How to read this lecture Everything here comes from one idea: a drawing is a computation with numbers. We start with two points $A,B$. A step draws a line or a circle through points we already have, and then takes an intersection. Taking an intersection means solving equations, and solving equations means adjoining numbers to a field.
  • §1 fixes the rules: what a ruler-and-compass construction actually is.
  • §2 puts coordinates on the picture. Points become complex numbers, and "can I construct this point?" becomes "is this number constructible?"
  • §3 proves the constructible numbers form a field closed under square roots.
  • §4 proves the main criterion: every step is at most quadratic, so a constructible number has degree a power of $2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.
  • §5–6 settle the three classical problems and describe the constructible regular polygons.
Read each worked question with paper and pencil: the figures are meant to be redrawn.

§1 The rules of the game

Preliminaries Mathematics is an axiomatic theory: axioms are assumed, every other statement must be proved from axioms and earlier results, and a proved statement is a theorem. In geometry we say "draw a line perpendicular to…". To justify it we give a ruler-and-compass construction and prove it is correct; once proved, it may be reused, like a theorem.
The ruler and compass have no markings: distances are never read off a scale. The only way a length enters the picture is through the compass: every point of the circle with centre $P$ through $Q$ is at the same distance $|PQ|$ from $P$.
Why only these five operations? A construction is a finite list of operations. With an unmarked ruler we can draw only straight lines, and with a compass only circles. So the only new points we can ever obtain are intersections of two such curves. This is why the list below is complete — there is nothing else a ruler and compass can do.
The five basic constructions Using points, lines and circles already constructed:
  • the line through two existing points;
  • the circle through one point with centre another point;
  • the intersection point of two non-parallel lines;
  • the one or two intersection points of a line and a circle;
  • the one or two intersection points of two circles.
Notation. $PQ$ is the segment, $|PQ|$ its length, $L(P,Q)$ the line through $P,Q$, and $C(P;Q)$ the circle with centre $P$ through $Q$.
Question 1 Why can a ruler-and-compass construction never produce a number "by measuring"? What replaces measuring?
Neither tool has a scale. Lengths are transferred only by drawing a circle: every point of $C(P;Q)$ is at distance $|PQ|$ from $P$. So lengths enter via circles and intersections only.

§2 Coordinates and constructible numbers

Setting up coordinates Choose two distinct points $A,B$; the line $AB$ is the x-axis. Draw $C(A;B)$ and $C(B;A)$; they meet in two points, and the line through them is the y-axis (the perpendicular bisector of $AB$). It meets the x-axis in the origin $O$. Declare $|OA|=1$, so $A=(1,0)$ and $B=(-1,0)$.
Why may we declare $|OA|=1$? A construction is scale-invariant: if we double the whole picture, the same operations still work. So choosing the unit costs nothing and gives us actual numbers to compute with.
Figure — setting up coordinates
The circles $C(A;B)$ and $C(B;A)$ have radius $2$ and meet at $P_2=(0,\sqrt3)$ and $P_3=(0,-\sqrt3)$. The line $L(P_2,P_3)$ is the y-axis (the perpendicular bisector of $AB$); it meets the x-axis at $O=(0,0)$.
Definitions — points and numbers (a) A point. A point $Z$ is constructible from $E,F,G,H$ if one of the following holds:
  • $Z\in L(E,F)\cap L(G,H)$, where $L(E,F)\neq L(G,H)$;
  • $Z\in L(E,F)\cap C(G;H)$;
  • $Z\in C(E;F)\cap C(G;H)$, where $C(E;F)\neq C(G;H)$.
(b) A constructible point. A point is constructible if it is $A$, or $B$, or the last point of a finite chain $P_1,\dots,P_n$ in which each $P_{i+1}$ is constructible from $\{A,B,P_1,\dots,P_i\}$.
(c) A complex number. $z=x+iy$ is constructible if the point $(x,y)$ is a constructible point.
(d) A real number. $x\in\mathbb{R}$ is constructible if the point $(x,0)$ on the x-axis is a constructible point — equivalently, if $x=x+0i$ is a constructible complex number.
(e) The set. $\mathbb{K}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}: z$ is constructible$\}$; the constructible real numbers are $\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$.
Important note Given a pair of constructible points, we do not assert that every point on the drawn line or on the drawn circles is constructible. Only the finitely many points explicitly obtained in the construction chain count.
Figure — a complex number as a point
$z=x+iy$ is constructible exactly when the point $(x,y)$ is constructible. For a real number $x$, the point is $(x,0)$ on the x-axis.
Question 2 Show $(0,1)$ is constructible, hence $1,-1,0,i\sqrt3,-i\sqrt3,i\in\mathbb{K}$.
The two circles $C(A;B)$, $C(B;A)$ meet at two points on the y-axis. How far from $O$?
  • $O=(0,0)$ is constructible (Figure above).
  • Both $P_2=(0,\sqrt3)$ and $P_3=(0,-\sqrt3)$ lie in $C(A;B)\cap C(B;A)$: each is at distance $2$ from both $A$ and $B$, so its height is $\sqrt{4-1}=\sqrt3$.
  • Hence $L(P_2,P_3)$ is the y-axis.
  • Then $(0,1)\in L(P_2,P_3)\cap C(O;A)$, and $C(O;A)$ has radius $1$.
So $(0,1)$ is constructible, and so are $i=(0,1)$, $1=(1,0)$, $-1=(-1,0)$, $0=(0,0)$, $\pm i\sqrt3=(0,\pm\sqrt3)$.
Figure — why $(0,1)$ is constructible
The y-axis is $L(P_2,P_3)$. The circle $C(O;A)$ has radius $1$, so it meets the y-axis exactly at $(0,1)$ and $(0,-1)$.
Lemma 1 $z=x+iy$ is constructible if and only if its real part $x$ and its imaginary part $y$ are constructible real numbers.
Question 3 Prove Lemma 1.
(⇒) Suppose $z=x+iy$ is constructible, i.e. $(x,y)$ is a constructible point.
  • Draw the vertical line through $(x,y)$ (parallel to the y-axis). It meets the x-axis at $(x,0)$, so $x$ is constructible.
  • Draw the horizontal line through $(x,y)$; it meets the y-axis at $(0,y)$.
  • The circle $C(O;(0,y))$ meets the x-axis at $(y,0)$ and $(-y,0)$: the compass has copied the length $|y|$ from the y-axis to the x-axis. So $y$ is constructible.
(⇐) Suppose $x$ and $y$ are constructible, so $(x,0)$ and $(y,0)$ are constructible points.
  • Move the length $|y|$ from the x-axis to the y-axis: $C(O;(y,0))$ meets the y-axis at $(0,y)$ and $(0,-y)$. So $(0,y)$ is constructible.
  • Draw the vertical line through $(x,0)$ and the horizontal line through $(0,y)$.
  • These lines are not parallel and meet at $(x,y)$. ∎
Why do we send $(0,y)$ to $(y,0)$ and back? The two axes store the same length in two different roles: $(y,0)$ is the length $y$ along the x-axis, while $(0,y)$ is the height $y$. To reach the point $(x,y)$ we need the height, i.e. the point $(0,y)$ on the y-axis — that is why we transfer.
  • Compass = length copier. $C(O;(y,0))$ meets the y-axis at $(0,y)$ and $(0,-y)$, because all of them are at distance $|y|$ from $O$. Nothing is measured; the circle simply rotates the segment $O(y,0)$ by $90^\circ$.
  • Back again. $C(O;(0,y))$ meets the x-axis at $(y,0)$ and $(-y,0)$. We use this in Question 5 to get $-a$ and in Question 7 to get $1/a$.
  • In short: a circle centred at $O$ moves a length freely between the two axes, and this is the only way lengths are ever moved.
Figure — transferring a length between the axes
One circle centred at $O$ carries the length $|y|$ from the x-axis to the y-axis (and back).

§3 $\mathbb{K}$ is a field closed under square roots

Definition — the set $\mathbb{K}$ $\mathbb{K}=\{\,z\in\mathbb{C} : z \text{ is constructible}\,\}$ is the set of constructible numbers, and $\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$ is the set of constructible real numbers.
By Lemma 1, $z\in\mathbb{K}$ exactly when $\operatorname{Re}z\in\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$ and $\operatorname{Im}z\in\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$. So $\mathbb{K}$ is completely described by its real part $\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$.
Theorem 1 The set $\mathbb{K}$ of constructible numbers satisfies:
  • $\mathbb{K}$ is a subfield of $\mathbb{C}$ (closed under $+,-,\times$ and division by nonzero elements);
  • $\mathbb{K}$ is closed under square roots: if $z\in\mathbb{K}$ then $\sqrt z\in\mathbb{K}$;
  • $\mathbb{K}$ is closed under complex conjugation: if $z\in\mathbb{K}$ then $\bar z\in\mathbb{K}$.
Strategy The theorem asks for three things at once. Lemma 2 breaks the job into two easier pieces:
  • (1) it is enough to check the field axioms for the real constructible numbers $\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$, because a complex number is just a pair of reals (Lemma 1);
  • (2) it is enough to construct $\sqrt a$ for positive real $a\in\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$, because for a complex $z=re^{i\theta}$ we then take $\sqrt r$ and bisect the angle $\theta$.
After Lemma 2 only five real constructions remain: $a+b$, $-a$, $ab$, $a^{-1}$, $\sqrt a$ (Questions 5–8).
Lemma 2 (reduction)
  • (1) If $\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$ is a subfield of $\mathbb{R}$, then $\mathbb{K}$ is a subfield of $\mathbb{C}$.
  • (2) If, in addition, $\sqrt a\in\mathbb{K}$ for every positive $a\in\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$, then $\mathbb{K}$ is closed under square roots.
Question 4 Prove Lemma 2.
For (1) use Lemma 1 and $z^{-1}=\bar z/(z\bar z)$. For (2) write $z=re^{i\theta}$ and bisect the angle.
Part (1). Let $z=a+ib$ and $w=c+id$ be in $\mathbb{K}$. By Lemma 1, $a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$. Since $\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$ is a subfield of $\mathbb{R}$:
  • $a+c,\ b+d\in\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$, hence $z+w=(a+c)+i(b+d)\in\mathbb{K}$, again by Lemma 1;
  • $ac-bd,\ ad+bc\in\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$, hence $zw=(ac-bd)+i(ad+bc)\in\mathbb{K}$;
  • $0,1\in\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$, so $0,1\in\mathbb{K}$, and $-z=(-a)+i(-b)\in\mathbb{K}$;
  • if $z\neq0$ then $a^2+b^2\in\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$ is nonzero, so $a/(a^2+b^2)$ and $-b/(a^2+b^2)$ lie in $\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$, and $$z^{-1}=\frac{a}{a^2+b^2}-i\,\frac{b}{a^2+b^2}\in\mathbb{K}.$$
Hence $\mathbb{K}$ is a subfield of $\mathbb{C}$.

Part (2). Let $0\neq z=a+ib\in\mathbb{K}$. Write $z=re^{i\theta}$.
  • $r^2=a^2+b^2\in\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$ and $r^2>0$;
  • by hypothesis $r=\sqrt{r^2}\in\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$, and then $\sqrt r\in\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$;
  • $e^{i\theta}=r^{-1}z\in\mathbb{K}$, since $\mathbb{K}$ is a field;
  • every angle can be bisected (see the next card), so $e^{i\theta/2}\in\mathbb{K}$;
  • therefore $\sqrt z=\sqrt r\,e^{i\theta/2}\in\mathbb{K}$. ∎
Angle bisection — statement and proof Claim. Every angle can be bisected with ruler and compass: if $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ is constructible, then so is $(\cos(\theta/2),\sin(\theta/2))$.
  • Let $O$ be the origin, $A=(1,0)$ and $P=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$. Then $|OA|=|OP|=1$, so $\triangle OAP$ is isosceles.
  • The perpendicular bisector of $AP$ passes through $O$ and bisects the angle $\angle AOP$.
  • Construct it: draw the two circles $C(A;P)$ and $C(P;A)$; either intersection point $Q$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AP$.
  • The line $OQ$ is the angle bisector.
  • Intersect $OQ$ with the unit circle $C(O;A)$ to get $(\cos(\theta/2),\sin(\theta/2))$. ∎
Figure — angle bisection
Since $|OA|=|OP|$, the perpendicular bisector of $AP$ passes through $O$ and is the angle bisector. The circles $C(A;P)$ and $C(P;A)$ construct that perpendicular bisector.
Caution — what Lemma 2 really needs The proof above uses Lemma 1 at every single step: a complex number is in $\mathbb{K}$ exactly when both of its coordinates are in $\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$. With this "rectangle" property, the field axioms are almost automatic. Without it, the hypothesis "$\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$ is a subfield of $\mathbb{R}$" is not enough, and the conclusion can fail:
  • $\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}\cup\mathbb{Q}i$: then $\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}=\mathbb{Q}$ is a subfield of $\mathbb{R}$, and $1\in\mathbb{K}$, $i\in\mathbb{K}$, but $1+i\notin\mathbb{K}$ — so $\mathbb{K}$ is not even closed under addition.
  • $\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)\cup\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)i$: $\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$ is a subfield, yet again $1+i\notin\mathbb{K}$.
  • $\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}\cup\mathbb{R}i$: $\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}=\mathbb{R}$ is a subfield, but $1+i\notin\mathbb{K}$.
In each case $\mathbb{K}$ is a "cross" and not a "rectangle": the point $(1,1)$ has both coordinates in $\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$, but the point itself is not in $\mathbb{K}$.
The example $\{0,i\}$ does not work: there $\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}=\{0\}$, which is not a subfield (it does not contain $1$), so the hypothesis fails. For the constructible numbers Lemma 1 supplies the rectangle property, so Lemma 2 is safe.
What remains By Lemma 2 it suffices to show the following, for $a,b\in\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$:
  • $a+b\in\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$ (Question 5);
  • $-a\in\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$ (Question 5);
  • $ab\in\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$ (Question 6);
  • $a^{-1}\in\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$ if $a\neq0$ (Question 7);
  • $\sqrt a\in\mathbb{K}\cap\mathbb{R}$ if $a>0$ (Question 8).
And $a-ib\in\mathbb{K}$ follows because $i\in\mathbb{K}$ and $\mathbb{K}$ is a field.
Question 5 Construct $-a$ and $a+b$ from $P=(a,0)$ and $Q=(b,0)$.
$-a$.
  • $(-a,0)$ is the other intersection of the x-axis with $C(O;P)$: the circle copies the length $|a|$ to the other side of $O$.
$a+b$.
  • Let $I=(0,1)$.
  • Let $Q'=(b,1)$, the intersection of the horizontal line through $I$ with the vertical line through $(b,0)$.
  • Draw the line through $Q'$ parallel to the line $IP$.
  • It meets the x-axis at $S=(a+b,0)$.
Why it works. The vector $\overrightarrow{IP}=(a,-1)$ equals the vector $\overrightarrow{Q'S}$, so the second line is simply the translate of $IP$ by $(b,0)$. Hence the x-coordinate of $S$ is $a+b$.
Figure — constructing $a+b$
$Q'=(b,1)$ is obtained from the horizontal line through $I$ and the vertical line through $(b,0)$. The line through $Q'$ parallel to $IP$ meets the x-axis at $S=(a+b,0)$, because $\overrightarrow{Q'S}=\overrightarrow{IP}=(a,-1)$.
Question 6 Construct $ab$ for $a,b>0$.
Use similar triangles $OAC$ and $OBD$ with $A=(1,0)$, $B=(1+a,0)$, $C=(0,b)$.
  • Let $A=(1,0)$, $B=(1+a,0)$ (constructible by Question 5) and $C=(0,b)$ (obtained by moving $b$ to the y-axis, as in Lemma 1).
  • Draw the line through $B$ parallel to the line $AC$; it meets the y-axis at a point $D$.
  • Now $\overrightarrow{BD}=(1+a)\cdot\overrightarrow{AC}$, because the two lines are parallel and the x-components are $1+a$ and $-1$ respectively.
  • Hence $\triangle OAC$ and $\triangle OBD$ are similar with ratio $1+a$, so $$\frac{|OD|}{|OC|}=\frac{|OB|}{|OA|}=1+a.$$
  • Therefore $|OD|=b(1+a)$ and $|CD|=|OD|-|OC|=ab$.
  • Since $b+ab$ and $-b$ are constructible, so is $ab$ (transfer $|CD|$ to the x-axis). ∎
Figure — constructing $ab$
$A=(1,0)$, $B=(1+a,0)$, $C=(0,b)$. The line through $B$ parallel to $AC$ meets the y-axis at $D$, and $|CD|=ab$.
Question 7 Construct $a^{-1}$ for $a>0$.
  • Let $I=(0,1)$, $P=(a,0)$ and $A=(1,0)$.
  • Draw the line through $A$ parallel to $IP$; its slope is $-1/a$.
  • It meets the y-axis at $(0,1/a)$.
  • Transfer this height to the x-axis with $C(O;\cdot)$, giving $(1/a,0)$. ∎
Question 8 Construct $\sqrt a$ for $a\ge0$.
Put $P=(1+a,0)$, take the midpoint $Q$ of $OP$, and draw the circle with diameter $OP$.
  • Let $A=(1,0)$ and $P=(1+a,0)$.
  • Construct the midpoint $Q$ of $OP$ (angle bisection of the straight angle at $O$, or the standard perpendicular-bisector construction).
  • Draw the circle $C(Q;O)$, which has diameter $OP$.
  • Let $R$ be its intersection with the vertical line through $A$.
  • The angle $\angle ORP$ is a right angle (angle in a semicircle), so the right triangles $AOR$ and $ARP$ are similar, and $$\frac{|OA|}{|AR|}=\frac{|AR|}{|AP|}.$$
  • Hence $|AR|^2=|OA|\cdot|AP|=1\cdot a=a$, so $|AR|=\sqrt a$. ∎
This completes the proof of Theorem 1.
Figure — constructing $\sqrt a$
Here $A=(1,0)$ and $P=(1+a,0)$, so $Q$ is the midpoint of $OP$. The vertical through $A$ meets the circle with diameter $OP$ at $R$, and $|AR|=\sqrt a$.
Corollary If $a,b,c$ are constructible ($a\ne0$), the roots of $ax^2+bx+c$ are constructible.
Question 9 Why? Is $\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}$ constructible?
  • The roots are $\dfrac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$, built from $\mathbb{K}$ by field operations and one square root (Theorem 1).
  • $\frac{1+\sqrt5}2$ is a root of $x^2-x-1$, so it is constructible.

§4 Constructible = tower of quadratic extensions

Lemma 3 Let $\mathbb{F}\subseteq\mathbb{C}$ be a field such that
  • $i\in\mathbb{F}$, and
  • $\mathbb{F}$ is closed under complex conjugation.
Let $z=a+ib,\ w=c+id\in\mathbb{F}$ and $P=(a,b),\ Q=(c,d)$. Then:
  • (1) $a,b\in\mathbb{F}$;
  • (2) if $L(P,Q)$ has equation $y=mx+q$ with $m,q\in\mathbb{R}$, then $m,q\in\mathbb{F}$;
  • (3) if $C(P;Q)$ has equation $(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2=r^2$, then $r^2\in\mathbb{F}$.
Question 10 Prove Lemma 3.
  • (1) $a=\tfrac12(z+\bar z)\in\mathbb{F}$ and $ib=\tfrac12(z-\bar z)\in\mathbb{F}$; since $i\in\mathbb{F}$, also $b\in\mathbb{F}$.
  • (2) The slope is $m=\dfrac{d-b}{c-a}\in\mathbb{F}$ (here we use that $\mathbb{F}$ is a field, so we may divide), and then $q=b-ma\in\mathbb{F}$.
  • (3) $r^2=(c-a)^2+(d-b)^2\in\mathbb{F}$. ∎
Lemma 4 (one step costs at most degree 2) Let $\mathbb{F}$ be as in Lemma 3, and let $P,Q,R,S$ have coordinates in $\mathbb{F}$. Then:
  • if $\alpha=u+iv\in L(P,Q)\cap L(R,S)$ with $L(P,Q)\neq L(R,S)$, then $[\mathbb{F}(\alpha):\mathbb{F}]\le2$;
  • if $\alpha\in L(P,Q)\cap C(R;S)$, then $[\mathbb{F}(\alpha):\mathbb{F}]\le2$;
  • if $\alpha\in C(P;Q)\cap C(R;S)$ with $C(P;Q)\neq C(R;S)$, then $[\mathbb{F}(\alpha):\mathbb{F}]\le2$.
Question 11 Prove Lemma 4, treating the three cases.
Case 3 reduces to Case 2 by subtracting the two circle equations.
  • Case 1 (two lines). The lines are $y=mx+q_1$ (or $x=b$) and $y=nx+q_2$ (or $x=c$), with coefficients in $\mathbb{F}$ by Lemma 3. They are not parallel, so solving the two linear equations gives $u,v\in\mathbb{F}$: the degree is $1$.
  • Case 2 (line and circle). Line $y=mx+q_1$ (or $x=b$), circle $(x-c)^2+(y-d)^2=r^2$, with $m,q_1,r^2\in\mathbb{F}$. Then $$r^2=(u-c)^2+(mu+q_1-d)^2,$$ so $u$ is a root of a quadratic over $\mathbb{F}\cap\mathbb{R}$, hence $[\mathbb{F}(u):\mathbb{F}]\le2$. Since $v=mu+q_1\in\mathbb{F}(u)$ and $i\in\mathbb{F}$, we get $\alpha=u+iv\in\mathbb{F}(u)$ and $[\mathbb{F}(\alpha):\mathbb{F}]\le2$.
  • Case 3 (two circles). $(u-a)^2+(v-b)^2=r^2$ and $(u-c)^2+(v-d)^2=s^2$ both read $u^2+v^2+\dots=0$. Subtracting gives $tu+t'v+t''=0$ with $t,t',t''\in\mathbb{F}$, which is a line; pairing it with one of the circles brings us back to Case 2. ∎
Theorem 2 (main criterion) A complex number $z$ is constructible if and only if there is a tower of fields $$\mathbb{Q}=\mathbb{K}_0\subset\mathbb{K}_1\subset\dots\subset\mathbb{K}_n$$ such that
  • $z\in\mathbb{K}_n$, and
  • $[\mathbb{K}_{j+1}:\mathbb{K}_j]\le2$ for every $j$.
Corollary. If $z$ is constructible, then $[\mathbb{Q}(z):\mathbb{Q}]=2^m$ for some $m\ge0$.
Question 12 Prove the "only if" direction, and deduce the Corollary.
  • Let $1,-1,z_1=i,z_2,\dots,z_n=z$ be a construction chain (we may start with $i$ because $i$ is constructible, Question 2).
  • Let $\mathbb{K}_j$ be the field generated over $\mathbb{Q}$ by $z_1,\dots,z_j$ and their conjugates. Then $\mathbb{K}_j$ contains $i$ and is closed under conjugation, so Lemma 4 applies to it.
  • Each $z_{j+1}$ is an intersection of two lines, a line and a circle, or two circles through points with coordinates in $\mathbb{K}_j$; by Lemma 4, $[\mathbb{K}_j(z_{j+1}):\mathbb{K}_j]\le2$.
  • The conjugate $\bar z_{j+1}$ is the corresponding intersection for the conjugated configuration, so adjoining it costs at most one more factor $2$.
  • Thus $[\mathbb{K}_n:\mathbb{Q}]$ is a power of $2$.
  • By the Tower Law (Lecture 1, Theorem 2), $[\mathbb{Q}(z):\mathbb{Q}]$ divides $[\mathbb{K}_n:\mathbb{Q}]$, hence it is a power of $2$.
  • Conversely, each quadratic step is a square root, which Theorem 1 constructs. ∎

Two natural questions about the size of $\mathbb{K}$

Question 13A — Is every quadratic extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ inside $\mathbb{K}$? Let $L/\mathbb{Q}$ be a quadratic extension. Is $L\subseteq\mathbb{K}$?
Every quadratic extension is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)$ with $d$ a squarefree integer. Where is $\sqrt d$?
Yes — every quadratic extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ is a subfield of $\mathbb{K}$.
  • A quadratic extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ has the form $L=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)$ with $d\in\mathbb{Z}$ squarefree, $d\neq1$.
  • $d$ is an integer, so $d\in\mathbb{Q}\subseteq\mathbb{K}$ (since $1\in\mathbb{K}$ and $\mathbb{K}$ is a field).
  • If $d>0$: $\sqrt d\in\mathbb{K}$ by Theorem 1.
  • If $d<0$: $|d|>0$, so $\sqrt{|d|}\in\mathbb{K}$, and $i\in\mathbb{K}$ (Question 2); hence $\sqrt d=i\sqrt{|d|}\in\mathbb{K}$.
  • Since $\mathbb{K}$ is a field containing $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\sqrt d$, it contains $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)=L$. ∎
Careful with the converse. It is not true that every element of $\mathbb{K}$ generates a quadratic extension of $\mathbb{Q}$: for example $\sqrt{1+\sqrt2}\in\mathbb{K}$ has degree $4$. And it is not true that $\mathbb{K}$ contains all algebraic numbers: $\sqrt[3]2\notin\mathbb{K}$ (Question 15).
Question 13B — Is $[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{Q}]$ finite or infinite?
Take repeated square roots of $2$. What are the degrees?
The extension is infinite: $[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{Q}]=\infty$.
  • Let $a_n=2^{1/2^n}$. Then $a_1=\sqrt2\in\mathbb{K}$, and if $a_n\in\mathbb{K}$ then $a_{n+1}=\sqrt{a_n}\in\mathbb{K}$ by Theorem 1. So $a_n\in\mathbb{K}$ for all $n$.
  • The minimal polynomial of $a_n$ is $x^{2^n}-2$: it is irreducible by Eisenstein at $p=2$.
  • Hence $[\mathbb{Q}(a_n):\mathbb{Q}]=2^n$.
  • Since $\mathbb{K}$ contains subfields of degree $2^n$ for every $n$, the degree $[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{Q}]$ cannot be finite. ∎
Two more facts (sketch).
  • $\mathbb{K}\subseteq\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$. Each construction step adjoins a number algebraic of degree $\le2$ over the previous field, so by induction every constructible number is algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$.
  • $\mathbb{K}\neq\mathbb{C}$. $\mathbb{K}\subseteq\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$ is countable, while $\mathbb{C}$ is uncountable.
  • $\mathbb{K}$ is not algebraically closed. $x^3-2$ has no root in $\mathbb{K}$, because $\sqrt[3]2$ has degree $3$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ (Question 15).
Figure — where $\mathbb{K}$ sits
$\mathbb{Q}\subsetneq\mathbb{K}\subsetneq\bar{\mathbb{Q}}\subsetneq\mathbb{C}$, with witnesses $\sqrt2\in\mathbb{K}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$, $\sqrt[3]2\in\bar{\mathbb{Q}}\setminus\mathbb{K}$, $\pi\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$. Each inclusion is strict, and $[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{Q}]=\infty$.

§5 Three impossible constructions

Question 14 — Squaring the circle Construct a square with the area of the unit circle, i.e. is $\sqrt\pi$ constructible?
  • No. By Lindemann (1882), $\pi$ is transcendental over $\mathbb{Q}$.
  • Hence $\sqrt\pi$ is transcendental too: otherwise $\pi=(\sqrt\pi)^2$ would be algebraic.
  • A transcendental number lies in no finite extension of $\mathbb{Q}$, so $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt\pi):\mathbb{Q}]=\infty$, not a power of $2$. ∎
Question 15 — Duplicating the cube Construct a cube of volume $2$: is $\alpha=\sqrt[3]2$ constructible?
  • No. $x^3-2$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ by Eisenstein with $p=2$.
  • Hence $[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]=3$, which is not a power of $2$. ∎
Question 16 — Trisecting an angle Show the angle $\pi/3$ (60°) cannot be trisected.
Let $a=\cos(\pi/9)$ and use $\cos3\theta=4\cos^3\theta-3\cos\theta$.
  • The point $(\cos\frac\pi3,\sin\frac\pi3)=(\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}2)$ is constructible.
  • If $60^\circ$ could be trisected, then $(a,b)=(\cos\frac\pi9,\sin\frac\pi9)$ would be constructible.
  • With $\theta=\pi/9$ in $\cos3\theta=4\cos^3\theta-3\cos\theta$: $4a^3-3a=\tfrac12$, so $8a^3-6a-1=0$.
  • Put $x=2a-1$. Then $8a^3-6a-1=f(2a-1)$ where $f(x)=x^3+3x^2-3$.
  • Eisenstein with $p=3$ shows $f$ is irreducible, so $[\mathbb{Q}(a):\mathbb{Q}]=[\mathbb{Q}(2a-1):\mathbb{Q}]=3$, not a power of $2$. ∎
Alternative. Put $y=2a$; then $y^3-3y-1=0$, which has no rational root $\pm1$, so it is irreducible and $[\mathbb{Q}(a):\mathbb{Q}]=3$.

§6 Regular polygons

Theorem 3 (Gauss) Let $p$ be an odd prime. Then:
  • a regular $p$-gon is constructible if and only if $p=2^{2^t}+1$ for some $t\ge0$;
  • such primes are called Fermat primes; the known ones are $3,5,17,257,65537$.
Theorem 4 A regular $n$-gon is constructible if and only if
  • $n$ is a power of $2$ times a product of distinct Fermat primes.

§8 SAQ — Short Answer Questions

Answer on paper first, then reveal.

SAQ 1Define a constructible real number.
$x\in\mathbb{R}$ is constructible if the point $(x,0)$ is a constructible point, i.e. if $x=x+0i\in\mathbb{K}$.
SAQ 2Define a constructible complex number.
$z=x+iy$ is constructible if the point $(x,y)$ is constructible; equivalently, if both $x$ and $y$ are constructible real numbers.
SAQ 3Name the operations under which $\mathbb{K}$ is closed.
$+,-,\times,\div$ (a subfield of $\mathbb{C}$), square roots, and complex conjugation.
SAQ 4State the main criterion for constructibility.
$z$ is constructible iff it lies in a tower $\mathbb{Q}=\mathbb{K}_0\subset\dots\subset\mathbb{K}_n$ with all $[\mathbb{K}_{j+1}:\mathbb{K}_j]\le2$.
SAQ 5If $z$ is constructible, what can $[\mathbb{Q}(z):\mathbb{Q}]$ be?
Only a power of $2$: $1,2,4,8,\dots$.
SAQ 6Why can't we trisect $60^\circ$?
$\cos20^\circ$ has minimal polynomial of degree $3$ (after scaling, $y^3-3y-1$), not a power of $2$.
SAQ 7Give a constructible number of degree $4$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.
$\sqrt{1+\sqrt2}$ (a tower of two quadratic steps; minimal polynomial $x^4-2x^2-1$), or $\sqrt2+\sqrt3$.
SAQ 8Is the regular 11-gon constructible?
No: $11$ is prime but $11-1=10$ is not a power of $2$, so $11$ is not a Fermat prime.
SAQ 9Is every quadratic extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ contained in $\mathbb{K}$?
Yes: every such extension is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)$ with $d$ a squarefree integer, and $\sqrt d\in\mathbb{K}$ (Theorem 1, plus $i\in\mathbb{K}$ when $d<0$).
SAQ 10Is $[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{Q}]$ finite?
No. $2^{1/2^n}\in\mathbb{K}$ has degree $2^n$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, and these degrees are unbounded, so $[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{Q}]=\infty$; moreover $\mathbb{Q}\subsetneq\mathbb{K}\subsetneq\bar{\mathbb{Q}}\subsetneq\mathbb{C}$.
